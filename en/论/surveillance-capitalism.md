# Essay · Surveillance Capitalism: From Data to Power

> When we use free internet services—Google search, Facebook social networking, YouTube videos—we are not "users" but "products." Our every click, every search, every pause is recorded, analyzed, and sold. This is not accidental—it is an economic model that Shoshana Zuboff calls "surveillance capitalism." The rise of AI has not changed this model—it has only made surveillance more precise, more concealed, and more pervasive.

---

Shoshana Zuboff's 2019 book *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* advances a core argument: at the turn of the 21st century, Google discovered a new mode of capital accumulation—**behavioral surplus**.[^1]

Google's search engine not only records users' search queries (which is necessary for "improving the service") but also records users' behavioral patterns—what they searched, what they clicked, how long they stayed, where, and when. This "behavioral surplus" is not used to improve the search experience—it is used to predict users' future behavior, and then sell those predictions to advertisers.

This model was quickly replicated by Facebook, Amazon, Microsoft, and other companies. By the 2020s, surveillance capitalism had become the default economic model of the internet.

---

## I. From Behavioral Surplus to AI Training Data

The rise of AI has a deep symbiotic relationship with surveillance capitalism.

Large models require massive amounts of training data. Where does this data come from? The answer: **the data infrastructure of surveillance capitalism.**

- **Common Crawl**: A nonprofit organization that periodically scrapes web pages across the internet, creating public web archives. Most large models—including GPT, LLaMA, and PaLM—are trained on Common Crawl data.[^2]
- **Google Books**: Google scanned over 25 million books between 2002 and 2011.[^3] These scanned texts have been used to train language models.
- **Social media data**: Public posts on Reddit, Twitter, and Facebook have been widely used for AI training.[^4]
- **YouTube videos**: Google uses YouTube video captions and audio to train AI models.[^5]

This means: **AI models' "intelligence" is built upon surveillance capitalism's "data."** Every search we have made, every click, every post on the internet over the past several decades—has been converted into AI training data.

We never consented to this. We did not know that our search queries would train ChatGPT, that our Facebook posts would train LLaMA, that our YouTube videos would train Gemini.

---

## II. The Double Exploitation of Data

The data collection of surveillance capitalism constitutes a **double exploitation.**

**First layer of exploitation**: Platforms collect user data for targeted advertising. Users receive "free" services at the cost of lost privacy. This is a tacit transaction—users have not explicitly agreed, but "using equals consenting."[^6]

**Second layer of exploitation**: Platforms use user data (or its derivatives) for AI training. Users' data is not only used to display advertisements but also to train AI models—models that may replace the users' own jobs.[^7]

A translator searches for translation materials on Google → Google records the search behavior → data is used to train translation AI → translation AI replaces the translator's job.

An illustrator posts work on Instagram → Instagram records user interactions → data is used to train image AI → image AI replaces the illustrator's job.

A programmer answers questions on Stack Overflow → Stack Overflow records the answers → data is used to train coding AI → coding AI replaces the programmer's job.

This is a **data blowback**: users' data is used to train the technologies that replace them.

---

## III. AI Accelerates Surveillance

AI is not just a "product" of surveillance capitalism—it is also a "tool" of surveillance capitalism.

**More precise surveillance:**

Traditional surveillance relies on coarse classifications—age, gender, geographic location, browsing history. AI can extract finer patterns from this coarse data—emotional states, political inclinations, consumer intentions, health conditions.[^8]

**More concealed surveillance:**

Traditional surveillance requires explicit data collection—cookies, trackers, login records. AI can infer sensitive information from seemingly unrelated data—inferring emotional states from typing speed, health conditions from gait, political inclinations from social networks.[^9]

**More comprehensive surveillance:**

Traditional surveillance can only cover the digital world—web browsing, app usage, online shopping. AI can extend surveillance to the physical world—facial recognition, behavioral analysis, predictive policing.[^10]

The UAE's Oyoon project is a textbook case: 300,000 cameras combined with AI facial recognition constitute a round-the-clock surveillance network.[^11] This network not only records what you are doing but also predicts what you are about to do.

---

## IV. The Price of "Free"

The internet's "free" model is the foundation of surveillance capitalism.

Google search is free. Facebook is free. YouTube is free. Gmail is free. But these "free" services have a price: your data.

This price is implicit. When you use Google search, you do not see a message saying: "This search will be recorded and used for commercial purposes." You only see a search box and search results. The process by which your data is collected is invisible.

AI makes this implicit price even more concealed. When you use ChatGPT, every prompt you input is recorded.[^12] OpenAI claims it uses this data to "improve services," but the specific meaning of "improving services" is vague. Your prompts may be used to train the next generation of models, to fine-tune existing models, or to develop new products—you don't know, and you have no control.

**The illusion of "consent":**

Internet services' "privacy policies" and "terms of use" are the legal infrastructure of surveillance capitalism. These documents are typically dozens of pages long, filled with legal jargon.[^13] According to a 2020 study, only 9% of users read privacy policies, and only 1% read terms of use.[^14]

"Consent" is an illusion. You did not truly read the privacy policy, you did not truly understand how your data would be used, and you have no real choice—either "consent" and use the service, or "refuse" and be excluded from the digital world.

---

## V. From Surveillance to Control

The ultimate goal of surveillance capitalism is not "surveillance"—but "control."

Zuboff calls this control **"instrumentarian power."**[^15] Unlike the violent control of totalitarianism, instrumentarian power achieves control through behavioral prediction and behavioral modification.

**Behavioral prediction**: By analyzing massive amounts of data, AI can predict what you are about to do—what you will buy, what you will vote for, what content you will watch, whom you will befriend.

**Behavioral modification**: Through precise content recommendations and interface design, platforms can influence your behavior—making you buy more things, watch more videos, spend more time on the platform.

**Electoral manipulation**: The Cambridge Analytica scandal (2018) revealed how surveillance capitalism could be used for political manipulation.[^16] This company used Facebook user data to construct psychological profiles, then used those profiles to deliver targeted political advertising.

AI makes behavioral modification more precise. Traditional advertising targeting is broad—based on age, gender, geographic location. AI-driven advertising targeting is precise—based on your psychological state, emotional disposition, consumer intent.[^17]

---

## VI. Is There a Way Out?

Are there alternatives to surveillance capitalism?

**Technical solutions:**
- Differential Privacy: Adding noise to data to protect individual privacy[^18]
- Federated Learning: Training AI models without sharing raw data[^19]
- Homomorphic Encryption: Performing computations directly on encrypted data[^20]

**Legal solutions:**
- EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR): Granting users rights to data access, deletion, and refusal[^21]
- EU Digital Markets Act (DMA): Restricting large platforms' data monopolies[^22]
- US California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA): Granting California residents rights similar to GDPR[^23]

**Business model solutions:**
- Paid instead of free: Users pay for services, platforms do not collect data
- Data cooperatives: Users collectively own and manage their own data
- Decentralized platforms: Blockchain-based decentralized social media

**Realistic assessment:**

Technical solutions are effective but limited—they cannot solve the problem of power asymmetry. Legal solutions are progressive but slow—GDPR's enforcement has limited effect. Business model solutions have promise but are difficult to scale—most users are unwilling to pay for "free" services.

Alternatives to surveillance capitalism require more than technical or legal change—they require **a redistribution of power.** When a handful of companies control most of the world's data and AI capabilities, any "solution" faces the dilemma of enforcement.

## Assessment

Surveillance capitalism is not a "technical problem"—it is a **problem of power.**

Who has the right to collect your data? Who has the right to use your data? Who has the right to profit from your data? The answers to these questions are not determined by technology, but by power.

Google has the right to collect your search data because it controls 90% of the global search market. Facebook has the right to collect your social data because it controls the social networks of 3 billion people worldwide. OpenAI has the right to use your chat data because it lets you use ChatGPT "for free."

What rights do you have? You can "consent" or "not consent." But when "not consenting" means being excluded from the digital world, "consent" is not truly consent—it is coercion.

The rise of AI makes surveillance capitalism even more powerful. Traditional surveillance can only record your past behavior; AI surveillance can predict your future behavior. Traditional surveillance can only affect your digital life; AI surveillance can affect your physical life—through facial recognition, behavioral analysis, predictive policing.

Opponents of surveillance capitalism—Zuboff, privacy advocates, digital rights organizations—have proposed various "solutions": GDPR, differential privacy, federated learning, data cooperatives. These solutions all have value, but they all face a fundamental dilemma: **they assume power can be constrained, but power will not constrain itself.**

Google will not stop collecting data because of GDPR—it will only change how it collects data. Facebook will not abandon surveillance because of user protests—it will only make surveillance more concealed. AI companies will not stop using training data because of public attention—they will only move to countries with weaker regulations.

The way out of surveillance capitalism is not more technology or more law—but **a redistribution of power.** When users own their data, own their privacy, and own their right to choose, surveillance capitalism will truly end.

But this goal is far from reality. Until that day arrives, we will continue living in a surveilled world—every search, every click, every conversation, recorded, analyzed, and exploited. (See "Essay · Data Colonialism.")
