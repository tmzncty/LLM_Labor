# Surveillance Capitalism and the Workplace: Do Not Collapse Every Algorithmic Tool into One Theory

OECD's 2025 six-country employer survey shows that algorithmic management is already widely used to coordinate, monitor and evaluate work, with recurring concerns about accountability, explainability and worker health.[^1]

That empirical fact is narrower than the theory of “surveillance capitalism.” The concept is most useful when behavioral data is systematically captured and converted into prediction and control. A writing assistant and a system that continuously scores worker behavior do not create the same power relation.

For labor, the practical questions are notice, data access, correction, human review, contestability and whether algorithmic outputs directly affect pay, scheduling, discipline or dismissal.

The EU's 2026 Quality Jobs Act consultation places algorithmic management and excessive monitoring directly on the labor-policy agenda.[^2]

This article therefore treats surveillance capitalism as an interpretive lens, not an empirical label for every AI deployment.
