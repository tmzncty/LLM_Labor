# Essay · Digital Labor: Who Pays for Your Likes

> How many hours a day do you spend on your phone? Scrolling through short videos, ordering takeout, posting on your social feed, searching for answers, liking posts—these activities look like "consumption," but they are simultaneously "production." Every click creates data for the platform, every swipe trains the recommendation algorithm, every post fills social media with content. You are not "using" the platform—you are working for it. The difference is: you have never received a paycheck. This is not a metaphor—it is a proposition in labor theory that academia is taking seriously. When you realize you are an "unpaid worker," the entire profit distribution logic of the digital economy needs to be re-examined.

---

## I. The Theoretical Lineage of Digital Labor: From Free Labor to Data Labor

"Digital labor" as an academic concept has its theoretical roots in the 2000 pioneering paper "Free Labor" by Italian-born scholar Tiziana Terranova.[^1] Terranova observed a paradox in the internet economy: users invested enormous time and energy creating content in chat rooms, forums, and newsgroups—moderators managed communities, users wrote reviews, programmers contributed open-source code—this labor created tremendous value, but received no compensation. She called this "free labor": **internet companies profit from users' unpaid contributions, and this profit relationship is obscured by positive discourses of "participation," "community," and "sharing."**

The internet of 2000 was primitive—no Facebook, no YouTube, no algorithmic recommendations. But Terranova's insight has not only not become obsolete twenty years later; it has become sharper. When Facebook uses your photos and posts to train algorithms, when YouTube uses your viewing history to optimize recommendations, when Google uses your search history for targeted advertising—these are contemporary forms of "free labor," only at several orders of magnitude greater scale.

Christian Fuchs, in his 2014 book *Digital Labour and Karl Marx*, integrated Terranova's observations into a Marxist analytical framework.[^2] Fuchs's core argument is: **social media user activity constitutes a form of labor—although it is not paid wages, it creates surplus value that is appropriated by capital.** Facebook (now Meta)'s business model illustrates this precisely: users freely create content (posts, photos, videos), the platform collects this content and user behavioral data, sells the data to advertisers, advertisers pay the platform—profits go to the platform, labor comes from users.

Fuchs introduced the concept of "prosumer"—users who are simultaneously producers and consumers.[^2] When you post on Facebook, you are both the producer of content (creating the platform's content supply) and the consumer of content (reading other users' posts). The platform's genius lies in: disguising "production" as "consumption." You think you're "playing on your phone"—in reality, you're working for the platform for free.

Shoshana Zuboff, in her 2019 book *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, deepened this analysis from another angle.[^3] Zuboff does not use the term "labor"—she uses "behavioral data" and "surveillance capitalism." Her core argument is: **platforms exploit not only users' conscious activities (posting, commenting) but also users' unconscious behaviors (dwell time, scroll speed, mouse trajectories) to produce "prediction products"—predictions about users' future behavior, sold to advertisers.**

Zuboff's analysis reveals a deeper problem: even if you don't "create" any content, merely "using" a platform is also a form of labor—because your behavioral data itself is a commodity. Your behavior of searching "headache medicine" is valuable—it tells advertisers you currently have a headache and may need to buy medicine. Your behavior of dwelling on a product page for 30 seconds is valuable—it tells the algorithm you are interested in that product. Your behavior of scrolling through short videos at 2 AM is valuable—it tells the platform you are a highly active user. **Your every digital behavior is being harvested, analyzed, and sold—and you neither know about it nor receive compensation.**

Nick Srnicek, in his 2017 book *Platform Capitalism*, integrated these observations into an industrial analysis framework.[^4] Srnicek categorizes platforms into four types: advertising platforms (Google, Facebook), cloud platforms (AWS, Azure), industrial platforms (GE, Siemens), and product platforms (Spotify, Netflix). The common feature of all platforms is: **they do not produce content; they own infrastructure—and through controlling infrastructure, they extract data rents.** The platform's core asset is not code, but users—because users provide free data input.

These four theoretical lineages—Terranova's free labor, Fuchs's digital labor, Zuboff's surveillance capitalism, Srnicek's platform capitalism—converge from different angles toward the same conclusion: **the profits of the digital economy are built upon massive unpaid labor, and this unpaid labor is obscured by narratives of "convenience," "free," and "personalization."**

## II. Users as Unpaid Workers: Your Every Click Is Labor

Calling "user activity" "labor" encounters intuitive resistance. "I'm just scrolling through videos—how can that count as labor?" This resistance itself is the most subversive aspect of the digital labor concept. If users realized they were laboring, they might demand compensation—which would be catastrophic for platforms. **The platform's top priority is ensuring that users never see themselves as workers.**

Let's do a thought experiment. Suppose a company hired hundreds of thousands of people to do these things: spend several hours each day taking photos, writing short texts, recording videos, then uploading them to a central server; simultaneously, this company also requires these employees to spend several hours each day browsing content uploaded by others, liking, commenting, and sharing, so the company can analyze what content is most popular. If this were a formal employment relationship, what compensation should these people receive? The answer depends on the market—but it clearly would not be zero.

Yet when the same activity occurs on social media platforms, it is no longer "labor"—it becomes "socializing," "entertainment," "self-expression." **The change of name eliminates the obligation of payment.** This is not a rhetorical game—it is the core mechanism of the digital economy. Platforms use the neutral term "User Generated Content" (UGC) to describe hundreds of millions of people's unpaid creation, as if this content fell from the sky rather than being written by humans.

Specifically, users' digital labor includes at least the following forms:

**Content creation labor.** Every Weibo post, every Instagram photo, every YouTube video, every Xiaohongshu review—is created by users investing time and energy. A carefully produced 60-second Douyin video may involve hours of filming and editing. A detailed Xiaohongshu product review may involve days of use experience. This content is the platform's core asset—without content, the platform is an empty shell. But content creators do not own the content—user agreements typically stipulate that the platform can "perpetually, irrevocably, and without charge" use all content uploaded by users.[^5]

**Social labor.** Your likes, comments, shares, follows—these "interaction" behaviors are the core fuel of recommendation algorithms. Algorithms need to know what content is popular, and the criterion is your interaction data. You are not merely "expressing liking"—you are training algorithms for free. When your like helps a video reach a million views, the video's creator (possibly) earned revenue, the platform earned advertising income—and you, only received a "liked" icon.

**Data labor.** Even if you never post, never comment, never like—merely "using" the platform, your behavioral data is being collected. Search history, browsing records, dwell time, scroll speed, geographic location, device information—this data is aggregated, analyzed, and sold for targeted advertising. Google's parent company Alphabet had advertising revenue exceeding $237 billion in 2024.[^6] The source of this revenue is not Google Search's technology—but the search behaviors of billions of users. **One search for "which restaurant is good nearby"—how much is this search behavior worth? Individually, almost zero. But when billions of searches are aggregated, it is worth $237 billion.** User data is raw material—mined, refined, and sold like crude oil.

**Attention labor.** This is the most concealed form. Platforms use recommendation algorithms to lock your attention onto the screen—every minute you spend on the platform is a minute that advertising can reach you. Advertising pricing is based on "cost per thousand impressions" (CPM)—your attention is quantified, priced, and sold. Meta's average revenue per user (ARPU) in North America is approximately $68/year.[^7] This means every hour you spend on Facebook and Instagram creates several cents of advertising revenue for Meta. You think you're "killing time"—the platform thinks you're "producing revenue."

These four forms of labor—content creation, social interaction, data generation, attention investment—constitute the "hidden labor foundation" of the digital economy. Their common feature is: **not recognized as labor, not compensated, not protected by labor laws, but creating enormous economic value.**

## III. How Platforms Extract Digital Labor: The Price of Free Services

"But platforms provide free services—you use Google search, Facebook social networking, YouTube videos for free. Isn't that a fair exchange?"

This is the most common defense, and the most carefully designed concealment.

First, "free" does not mean "without cost." Google search is not free—you paid with your search behavioral data. Facebook is not free—you paid with your social data and attention. The function of the "free" label is: **to make you forget what you are paying.** When you see "free sample" in a supermarket, you know it's a promotional strategy. But when Google tells you "search is free," you do not realize you are paying with data—because data, unlike money, is not tangible.

Zuboff calls this mechanism the extraction of "behavioral surplus."[^3] The platform's services (search, social, video) are a "lure"—their function is to attract you into generating more behavioral data. This data exceeds what is needed to improve the service—this portion of "surplus" is used by the platform to produce "prediction products," sold to advertisers. The search service is bait; behavioral data is the prey. You think you are using a tool—in reality, the tool is using you.

Second, user agreements are unilateral contracts of rights transfer. Nearly all social media platforms require users to sign user agreements granting the platform broad rights over user content—including the rights to copy, modify, distribute, publicly display, and create derivative works.[^5] These agreements are typically thousands of words long, written in legal jargon, designed so that almost no one reads them—but they constitute legally valid "consent." When you register a social media account, you clicked "I agree"—you surrendered most of your rights over your creative content. **This "consent" is formally voluntary but substantively coercive—because not clicking "agree" means being excluded from the digital public sphere.**

Third, the core of the platform economy is network effects—they compel users to "voluntarily" provide labor. A social network without friends on it is worthless. When everyone is on WeChat, not being on WeChat equals social isolation. When all merchants are on Meituan, a restaurant not on Meituan essentially does not exist. Network effects create a pressure to "have to participate"—you can "choose" not to use WeChat, but the cost of this choice is disconnecting from nearly all of society. **When the cost of "choice" becomes too high to bear, "choice" ceases to be free.**

The commodification of data deserves unpacking. Platforms collect raw behavioral data from users—search terms, click sequences, dwell times, geographic locations. This raw data has limited value in itself. But when platforms aggregate the behavioral data of billions of users and analyze it with machine learning algorithms, they can produce high-value "prediction products": which users might buy sneakers? Which users might be interested in credit cards? Which users might have travel plans in the near future? These predictions are sold to advertisers, who use them for targeted advertising.

**Your data is not worth much—but you and a billion people's data together are worth hundreds of billions of dollars.** This value leap from individual to aggregate is the secret of platform profits: individual users have no bargaining power ("how much is my data alone worth? Almost zero"), but the platform has the power to aggregate all data ("how much is everyone's data together worth? An astronomical figure"). This is the core inequality of the platform economy—**the individual who creates value gets zero; the platform that aggregates value gets everything.**

## IV. Annotators as the "Digital Proletariat": A Continuum from Data to Labor

If discussions of digital labor stop at "users are exploited," they miss a sharper reality: in the labor chain of the digital economy, there exists a continuum from "unpaid" to "low-paid." Users contribute unpaid data labor; annotators contribute extremely low-paid judgment labor; content moderators contribute high-psychological-trauma moderation labor. These three forms of labor together constitute the "labor foundation" of AI and the platform economy—but in public narratives, all three are almost completely invisible.

(See detailed analysis in "Investigation · Kenyan Labelers" and "Essay · Labor Justice.")

Annotation work is essentially the "physicalization of digital labor"—it transforms data and content generated by users in digital spaces into training signals that AI models can use. Users post on social media—content moderators determine whether posts violate rules; users enter queries in search engines—annotators determine the relevance of search results; users converse with AI—RLHF annotators evaluate the quality of AI responses. **Annotators are the missing middle link in the digital labor chain—they are neither "free" like users nor "high-paid" like engineers—they are the "digital proletariat" of the digital economy.**

The existence of this continuum reveals a blind spot in digital labor theory: if the focus is only on "whether users are exploited," it overlooks those who work for digital infrastructure and are more directly exploited. Users at least "enjoy" the services provided by the platform—annotators do not even have this "enjoyment." What they face is not the "implicit cost of free services" but "explicit, low-paid, high-harm labor."

This continuum also exposes an internal tension in digital labor theory. Terranova's and Fuchs's analyses focused on "users' unpaid labor"—this analysis generated extensive discussion in Western academia. But when the same analytical framework is applied to "annotators' low-paid labor," the discussion's temperature drops sharply. Why? Because users' unpaid labor occurs in the context of "consumption"—it evokes "being deprived of deserved compensation" and easily generates sympathy. Annotators' low-paid labor occurs in the context of "employment"—it evokes "low-skill work deserves low pay" and is easily rationalized. **Moral sensitivity to digital labor is inversely proportional to the labor's visibility—the more concealed the form of labor, the more easily it is taken for granted.**

Another neglected fact: digital labor's value creation involves enormous time delays. Users' data labor today may not be used to train a new model until years later—and the value of that model can never be retroactively claimed by the user. A YouTube creator's video uploaded in 2018 may be used to train a video generation model in 2024—but the creator is completely unaware and receives no compensation. **The delay of time dissolves the visibility of causality—you cannot prove "my data labor contributed to this model" because you do not even know which model used your data.** This temporal discontinuity makes the retrospective tracing of digital labor's value nearly impossible in both technology and law—and this happens to benefit platforms.

Placing users and annotators within the same analytical framework reveals a complete chain of exploitation: **users provide data raw materials for free → platforms aggregate data and produce prediction products → AI companies use annotators' low-paid labor to train models → models further automate annotation and moderation work → annotators are abandoned, users' data is exploited more efficiently.** Every link in this chain extracts value from humans, and the compensation at every link diminishes—from zero to near zero.

## V. Prospecting Solutions: Data Cooperatives, UBI, Digital Unions, Platform Cooperativism

If digital labor is a genuinely existing form of labor, then workers should receive compensation—this inference is logically sound but faces enormous practical difficulties.

**Proposal 1: Data Cooperatives.** The core idea is for users to collectively own their data and negotiate data usage prices with platforms as a collective entity. Just as unions represent workers in negotiations with employers, data cooperatives represent users in negotiations with platforms—replacing individual powerlessness with collective bargaining. Theoretically attractive—but with enormous practical obstacles: users are scattered globally, data value is difficult to measure, and platforms can threaten users with "if you don't join, you can't use the service." Mediachain and Solid (a project led by Tim Berners-Lee) attempted to build decentralized data ownership frameworks, but have not achieved widespread adoption to date.[^8]

**Proposal 2: Universal Basic Income (UBI).** If AI and automation render large amounts of labor "unnecessary for humans," then income distribution can no longer depend entirely on the "labor-wage" exchange relationship. UBI's logic is: since everyone's data and attention create value for the digital economy, everyone should receive a basic income from this value. This proposal is theoretically elegant—but faces two fundamental problems: where does the money come from (taxes? data taxes? platform profit sharing?), and how much counts as "basic."[^9] Sam Altman is a prominent advocate for UBI—but the company he leads is precisely one of the largest beneficiaries of digital labor. When the exploiter advocates compensation, we have reason to ask: is this sincerity, or a stalling tactic?

**Proposal 3: Digital Unions.** Traditional unions represent workers in collective bargaining with employers. The digital union concept extends this model to the digital labor domain—representing users and annotators in collective bargaining with platforms. But digital unions face a structural dilemma: users are not "employees," and their "employer" is a platform—the platform can modify algorithms, change rules, and ban accounts at any time, while users have no legal protection. A few attempts—such as Germany's IG Metall representing "crowdworkers" in collective bargaining—have achieved limited results.[^10] But overall, digital unions remain at the conceptual stage.

**Proposal 4: Platform Cooperativism.** Trebor Scholz and Nathan Schneider proposed this alternative in 2016: instead of letting platforms exploit users, let users own the platforms themselves.[^11] Platform cooperatives are collectively owned and governed by users—profits belong to users, data belongs to users, rules are made by users. Theoretically the most thorough—but in practice, platform cooperatives struggle to compete with capital-driven platforms (no venture capital support, slow growth, limited technical capabilities). Mastodon (a decentralized social network) and Stocksy (a photographer-owned image library) are among the few successful cases—but their scale is negligible compared to Facebook or Instagram.

All four proposals are attractive, and all have fatal weaknesses. Together they point to a more fundamental problem: **the problem of digital labor is not that "no solution can be found" but that "those with vested interests have no incentive to adopt solutions."** Platforms profit from free labor and have strong incentives to maintain the status quo. Users are numbed by "free" and "convenient" and have no incentive for collective action. Annotators are at the bottom of the labor chain and have no voice. Government regulation of the digital economy lags far behind technological development.

The deeper dilemma is: the "unpaid nature" of digital labor is not a "bug" that can be easily fixed—it is the "feature" of the platform economy. If Facebook had to pay users for every post, it would go bankrupt. If Google had to pay users for every search, it would no longer be Google. **Free labor is not a byproduct of the platform economy—it is the platform economy's foundation.** Remove this foundation, and the entire business model needs to be rebuilt. This is why this problem is so difficult to solve—because the cost of the solution is the collapse of the existing profit structure.

Is it technically possible for users to profit from their own data? Theoretically—blockchain project Brave Browser returns a portion of advertising revenue to users in token form, but actual user income is negligible, and the model has never achieved mainstream adoption. The fundamental obstacle is: when an individual user's data is worth near zero, any "return" mechanism gives the individual a return that approaches zero.

## Assessment

This diviner projects three rounds.

**First round: the conceptual level.** "Digital labor" is a valid theoretical framework. From Terranova's "free labor" to Fuchs's "digital labor" to Zuboff's "surveillance capitalism" to Srnicek's "platform capitalism"—four theoretical lineages converge from different angles toward the same judgment: the digital economy's profits are built upon massive unpaid labor. This is not a radical left-wing slogan—it is a structural analysis of platform business models. When over 98% of Facebook's revenue comes from advertising, and advertising is based on user data, saying "users are working for Facebook for free" is not a metaphor—it is a factual description.

**Second round: the evidence level.** Users' content creation, social interaction, data generation, and attention investment—each creates quantifiable economic value. Meta's North American ARPU is approximately $68/year; Google's 2024 advertising revenue exceeded $237 billion. The source of these revenues is billions of users' unpaid activities. "Free" is not an economic state—it is a narrative strategy designed to make users forget what they are paying. User agreements are the legal guarantee of this forgetting—a document thousands of words long, almost no one reads, but legally valid as "consent."

**Third round: the justice level.** This is the core projection of this essay.

The problem of digital labor is not simply that "users are exploited"—that judgment is too simple. The problem lies in a more precise structure: **there is a systematic disconnection between those who create value and those who obtain value.** Users create data; platforms obtain profits. Annotators contribute judgment; AI companies obtain valuations. Content creators contribute the foundation of the attention economy; platforms take advertising revenue. This disconnection is not a "market failure"—it is the result of market rules being deliberately designed.

Echoing the analysis in "Essay · Labor Justice"—digital labor sits at one end of a labor continuum from "unpaid" to "low-paid." Users are the unpaid end, annotators are the low-paid end, and workers displaced by AI are yet another end—"once paid, now unpaid." Together, the three constitute the "hidden labor foundation" of the digital economy—but in public narratives, all three are invisible.

"Free" is the most exquisite tool of exploitation. It eliminates the perception of "labor"—if I paid money, I know I'm exchanging; if I didn't pay, I assume I'm a "user" rather than a "worker." **The invisibility of digital labor is not an information gap—it is a precondition for the platform economy's survival.** If every user realized they were working for the platform for free, the platform economy's legitimacy would collapse.

And annotators—as the "physicalization of digital labor"—demonstrate a sharper reality: even if your labor is not recognized as "labor," even if your labor is not paid wages, your labor is still being consumed. You simply cannot see the path of consumption. Every video you swipe on Douyin is optimizing the recommendation algorithm; optimization of the recommendation algorithm increases the demand for data annotation; data annotation work is completed by workers earning less than two dollars per hour. **Your "entertainment" and his "labor" are connected through an algorithmic chain—but you will never see him, and he will never see you.** This mutual invisibility is the digital economy's most effective security mechanism.

The question ultimately returns to an ancient inquiry: **who should define the value of labor?** If the answer is "the market," then users' data labor is worth zero, because users "voluntarily" provide it for free. If the answer is "contribution," then users' data labor is worth billions, because without it there would be no platform profits. There is a vast chasm between the market's definition and the contribution's definition—and the entire source of platform profits lies within that chasm.

Digital labor is not a technical problem that can be "solved"—it is a political problem that must be "faced." Data cooperatives, UBI, digital unions, platform cooperativism—every proposal points in the same direction: **returning the value of labor to the laborer.** But every proposal's implementation requires challenging existing power structures—and those with vested interests will not voluntarily yield.

What the historian needs to record is: when future historians look back at this era, they will not ask "how smart was AI." They will ask—**when billions of people were working for platforms for free, when their attention, data, and creativity were systematically converted into profits, when all of this was obscured by narratives of "free" and "convenient"—why did no one question it?**

The answer is: someone did question it. But the voice was too quiet.

And what this diviner can do is to record these projections on the record—drawing a visible map of the invisibility of digital labor.
