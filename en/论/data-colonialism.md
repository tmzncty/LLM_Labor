# Essay · Data Colonialism: The New Plunder of the AI Age

> The history of colonialism is a history of plunder—from the gold and silver of the Americas to the ivory of Africa to the oil of the Middle East. Each time, core nations extracted resources from peripheral nations and brought the profits home. The colonialism of the AI age plunders a different resource: **data** (see "Essay · The Global South," "Essay · Surveillance Capitalism"). Workers in the Global South are not only hired at minimum wages to annotate data—their own data—search histories, social media, biometrics—is also collected and exploited without conditions.

---

## I. From Resource Colonialism to Data Colonialism

The core logic of colonialism is: **extract resources from the periphery, process them in the core, and keep profits in the core.**

16th–19th century colonialism: extracting gold, silver, and cotton from the Americas, processing them into finished goods in Europe, profits remaining in Europe.

20th century neocolonialism: extracting oil from the Middle East, processing it into chemical products and energy in the West, profits remaining in the West.

21st century data colonialism: extracting data from the globe, training AI models in Silicon Valley, profits remaining in Silicon Valley.

**Similarities:**
- Extraction of resources (data) does not require local consent
- Processing (AI training) occurs in core nations
- Profit distribution is severely asymmetric
- Peripheral nations are locked into the role of "raw material suppliers"

**Differences:**
- Traditional colonialism required military force; data colonialism does not—it operates through "free" services
- Traditional colonialism's resources were finite; data colonialism's "resources" are infinitely generated
- Traditional colonialism's victims knew their resources were being plundered; data colonialism's victims usually do not

## II. The Three Layers of Data Plunder

### Layer 1: The Plunder of Behavioral Data

Internet users in the Global South, like those in the North, use Google, Facebook, YouTube, and WhatsApp. These platforms record users' every search, every click, every pause.[^1]

But users in the Global South face a special dilemma: **asymmetry of digital infrastructure.**

In countries like Kenya, the Philippines, and India, many internet users' "internet" is Facebook or WhatsApp. Meta's Free Basics project in these countries offers "free" internet service—but with access only to Facebook and a handful of websites.[^2]

This means: users' data in these countries is monopolized by Meta. They have no alternative—either use Meta's "free" services and surrender their data, or not use the internet at all.

### Layer 2: The Plunder of Labor Data

Annotators not only sell their labor at minimum wages—the data generated during their work is also collected and exploited by platforms.

When an annotator completes a task on Remotasks, the platform records not only the task result but also:[^3]
- Annotation speed (how quickly a task is completed)
- Annotation accuracy (what the error rate is)
- Annotation patterns (which part of an image is habitually looked at first)
- Working hours (when online, how long worked)

This data is used to optimize worker efficiency—making workers faster, more accurate, and more persistent. This is another form of "data exploitation": workers' behavioral data is used to optimize workers' labor, while workers share none of the optimization's gains.

### Layer 3: The Plunder of Biometric Data

The most concealed plunder is the plunder of **biometric data.**

AI models require massive amounts of facial data to train facial recognition systems. Where does this data come from? Partly from public facial datasets—and these datasets contain facial images of large numbers of people from the Global South.[^4]

IBM's Diversity in Faces dataset (2019) contained millions of face photos scraped from Flickr, many from developing countries.[^5] The subjects of these photos were not notified, were not asked for consent, and received no compensation.

Facial recognition training data has another source: **content moderation.** When annotators in Kenya review images containing faces, they are effectively providing annotation data for facial recognition systems.[^6]

## III. The Institutional Foundations of Data Colonialism

Data colonialism does not happen by accident. It has institutional foundations.

**Asymmetry of intellectual property:**

The intellectual property of AI models belongs to AI companies. But the data used to train AI models comes from the global internet—from billions of users' creations, searches, and social interactions.[^7]

This means: AI companies use global data to train their own models, then sell those models back to global users. The creators of the data (global users) receive no compensation, while the users of the data (AI companies) receive enormous profits.

**Platform terms of service:**

The terms of service of platforms like Google, Facebook, and OpenAI all contain a key clause: **users grant the platform broad rights to use their data.**[^8]

For example, OpenAI's terms of use stipulate that content input into ChatGPT may be used for model training. Users can choose to "opt out," but the default is "consent."

This "opt-out" rather than "opt-in" mechanism ensures that platforms can legally collect and use the vast majority of users' data.

**Dependency on technical infrastructure:**

The Global South's digital infrastructure is highly dependent on American companies.[^9]
- Search: Google holds 90%+ of global market share
- Social: Facebook (Meta) dominates the Global South
- Cloud services: AWS (Amazon), Azure (Microsoft), GCP (Google) control most of the global cloud market
- AI: GPT (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic), Gemini (Google) are the most widely used AI models globally

This dependency means: nearly all of the Global South's data is stored on servers controlled by American companies, processed by algorithms developed by American companies, and used to train models owned by American companies.

## IV. The Possibility of Resistance

Can data colonialism be resisted?

**Legal pathways:**

- **GDPR** (EU): Granting users rights to data access, deletion, and refusal[^10]
- **LGPD** (Brazil): A data protection law similar to GDPR[^11]
- **POPI** (South Africa): Personal Information Protection Act[^12]

But the enforcement of these laws faces a fundamental problem: **jurisdiction.** When data is stored on American servers, how can Brazilian or South African law be enforced?

**Technical pathways:**

- **Data localization**: Requiring data to be stored within a country's borders[^13]
- **Open-source AI**: Developing nonprofit open-source AI models to reduce dependence on American companies[^14]
- **Data cooperatives**: Users collectively owning and managing their own data[^15]

**Political pathways:**

- **Digital sovereignty**: Countries developing their own digital infrastructure and AI capabilities[^16]
- **International cooperation**: Establishing a global data governance framework[^17]
- **UN framework**: Discussing data justice at the United Nations level[^18]

---

## V. Toward Data Justice

What is the alternative to data colonialism?

One possibility is **data justice**—a framework that applies principles of justice to the collection, use, and distribution of data.[^19]

Core principles of data justice include:
- **Consent**: The collection and use of data must be subject to informed consent
- **Fairness**: The value generated by data should be distributed fairly
- **Accountability**: Users of data must be held accountable for how data is used
- **Transparency**: The flow and uses of data must be traceable

These principles are simple in theory but difficult in practice. Because the beneficiaries of data colonialism—AI companies, platform companies, investors—possess enormous political and economic power. They will not voluntarily surrender their profits.

## Assessment

The concept of "data colonialism" may make people uncomfortable. It implies a moral judgment: the global flow of data is not the result of a "free market" but the result of "exploitation."

But the facts are: the global flow of data does indeed involve severe asymmetries.

American companies collect global data → train AI models in America → sell AI products back to the globe → profits remain in America.

Every step in this chain involves asymmetries of power. Users in the Global South have no real choice—either use American platforms or be excluded from the digital world. Governments in the Global South have no real jurisdiction—data is stored on American servers, protected by American law. Workers in the Global South have no real bargaining power—their wages are determined by global labor market supply and demand.

This asymmetry is not the result of "free choice." It is the result of "no choice."

Opponents of data colonialism might point out: users in the Global South do derive value from American platforms—search services, social networks, AI assistants. These services are valuable, and users "paying" with data is a fair transaction.

This argument has a fatal flaw: **the terms of the transaction were not negotiated as equals.** Users did not participate in drafting the terms of service, had no say over the scope and manner of data usage, and do not share in the value generated by the data. This is not a "transaction"—it is "take it or leave it."

Where is the way out of data colonialism?

The answer is uncertain. But one thing is clear: if the Global South cannot develop its own digital infrastructure and AI capabilities, data colonialism will persist.

Open-source AI is a promising direction. Meta's LLaMA, Mistral's models, DeepSeek's models—these open-source models allow developers in the Global South to develop AI applications without depending on American companies.

But open-source AI only solves the problem of technological dependency; it does not solve the problem of data dependency. Training AI models still requires data, and data still primarily comes from platforms controlled by American companies.

The ultimate realization of data justice requires more than technical or legal change—it requires **a change in the global power structure.** Until that day arrives, data colonialism will continue to exist—concealed, systematic, and invisible.
