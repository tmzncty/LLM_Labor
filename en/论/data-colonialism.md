# Data Colonialism: A Useful Warning Only When Control Is Specified

“Data colonialism” compares modern data extraction with longer histories of resource appropriation. It can illuminate cross-border power, but without a concrete mechanism it becomes rhetoric.

Audited AI labor cases show that annotation, moderation and expert feedback can be outsourced through multilayer vendor chains; workers often do not own the resulting data, rubrics or model improvements, and may not know the ultimate client.

These facts support analysis of asymmetric power and value capture. They do not automatically prove a colonial relation.

Use the framework only when specific mechanisms are shown: weak consent, unilateral pricing power, cross-border extraction of knowledge/data, externalized risk, concentrated ownership, or local inability to govern downstream use.

AI data is also produced through labor. Treating it as a natural resource can erase the people who write, judge, label and rank it.

This article therefore treats data colonialism as a critical lens, not a legal finding or default label.

Reference: Nick Couldry & Ulises A. Mejias, *The Costs of Connection* (2019). Empirical base: the archive's revised Africa supply-chain, Scale AI and MTurk investigations.
