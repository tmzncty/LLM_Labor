# Blind Spots in AI Ethics: Audit Omissions Instead of Assuming Hypocrisy

The old article claimed AI ethics was deliberately designed to be incomplete. That is an unsupported claim about motive.

UNESCO's AI ethics recommendation explicitly centers human rights, dignity, fairness, transparency and human oversight, so it is inaccurate to say mainstream AI ethics ignores society entirely.[^1]

The real limitation is that broad principles do not automatically answer employment questions: who is the employer in a subcontracting chain, who can appeal account deactivation, whether algorithms may determine scheduling or discipline, how digital replicas are compensated, how moderation injuries are handled, or how productivity gains are shared.

ILO work in 2025 explicitly addresses occupational exposure, job quality, social dialogue and data laborers.[^2] OECD research examines accountability, explainability and worker-health risks in algorithmic management.[^3] The EU's Quality Jobs process goes further into workplace rules.

A better audit asks whether an ethics framework identifies supply-chain workers, provides enforceable review and responsibility, addresses contracts and health, and makes governance costs visible.

Critique omissions and weak enforcement where evidence supports them; do not infer deliberate hypocrisy without evidence.
