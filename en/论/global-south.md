# Essay · The Global South: Colonial Structures in the AI Supply Chain

> AI's "intelligence" is a product of globalization: data comes from the global internet, computing power comes from American data centers, training data annotation comes from workers in Kenya and the Philippines, and product profits return to Silicon Valley. This is not a simple "division of labor"—it is a pyramid structure where profits concentrate upward and risks transfer downward. The Global South's role in this structure is: to provide the cheapest labor, bear the greatest risks, and receive the least reward. This is not a new story—it is the digitized version of colonialism.

---

The globalized layout of the AI industry is not the result of free market choice. It is an engineered structure serving specific interests.

When we talk about "AI supply chain," we typically mean: chip design (United States) → chip manufacturing (Taiwan) → cloud services (United States) → model training (United States) → application development (global) → users (global). This is a linear chain from upstream to downstream.

But this chain overlooks a critical link: **data annotation**. Data annotation does not sit between "chip design" and "model training"—it is hidden deeper. It is the foundation, but no one looks at the foundation.

---

## I. The Pyramid of Profit

The profit distribution of the AI industry is a precise pyramid.

**Top tier: AI model companies.** OpenAI is valued at $86 billion (2024), Google DeepMind has a research and development budget of billions of dollars, and Meta AI's laboratory employs thousands of researchers. These companies define the direction, standards, and rules of AI. They take the largest share of profits.[^1]

**Middle tier: infrastructure companies.** NVIDIA monopolizes the AI training chip market, Microsoft and Amazon provide most cloud services, and companies like Scale AI supply annotated data. These companies take the middle layer of profits.[^2]

**Bottom tier: annotation workers.** Annotators in Kenya earn $1.32 per hour, crowdsourcing workers in the Philippines earn $0.02 per image, and engineers in Venezuela are forced to switch to data annotation (see "Investigation · Kenyan Labelers," "Investigation · ScaleAI's Outsourcing System"). They create the "fuel" of AI but receive almost nothing themselves.[^3]

The structural characteristic of this pyramid is: **profits flow upward, risks flow downward.**

When AI companies achieve valuations of hundreds of billions, annotator wages do not rise. When NVIDIA's stock price soars, Filipino workers' lives do not improve. When Scale AI's founder becomes a billionaire, Remotasks annotators are still struggling on the poverty line.

## II. The Inverted Pyramid of Risk

The pyramid of profit points upward, but the pyramid of risk points downward.

**AI companies' risks:**
- Model failure (low probability, because it can be iterated)
- Competitive failure (moderate probability, but with sufficient capital buffer)
- Regulatory risk (low to moderate, because AI companies have strong lobbying power)

**Annotation workers' risks:**
- Mental health risk (high probability: PTSD, anxiety, depression—see "Investigation · The Psychological Cost of Data Annotation")[^4]
- Economic risk (high probability: unstable income, replaceable at any time)
- Career risk (high probability: non-transferable skills, inability to accumulate human capital)
- Legal risk (moderate probability: restrictions from confidentiality agreements, absence of labor rights)

**Comparison:** A Silicon Valley AI engineer and a Kenyan annotator face entirely different types of risk. The engineer's risk is "how much are my stock options worth"; the annotator's risk is "can I earn enough money today to feed my family."

This asymmetry in risk distribution is a structural feature of the AI industry. It is not an accident but design—outsourcing the dirtiest, most dangerous work to the most distant places, employing at the lowest cost those with the least bargaining power.

## III. The Structure of Neocolonialism

The globalized layout of the AI supply chain bears a striking structural resemblance to 19th-century colonialism.

**19th-century cotton supply chain:**
- Southern plantations (periphery): producing raw materials (cotton), using slave labor
- Northern factories (core): manufacturing finished goods (textiles), capturing profits
- Profit flow: from periphery to core
- Risk flow: from core to periphery

**21st-century AI supply chain:**
- Global South (periphery): providing labor (data annotation), using low-wage crowdsourcing
- Silicon Valley (core): producing finished goods (AI models), capturing profits
- Profit flow: from periphery to core
- Risk flow: from core to periphery

**Similarities:**
- **Asymmetric power relations:** The core defines the rules; the periphery executes them
- **Invisibility of labor:** Slaves in plantations did not appear on textile labels; annotators do not appear in AI model technical papers
- **Concentration of profits:** The cotton supply chain's profits were concentrated among northern factory owners; the AI supply chain's profits are concentrated among Silicon Valley companies

**Differences:**
- **Coercion vs. voluntariness:** Slaves had no choice; annotators nominally have a choice
- **Visible vs. invisible:** Slavery has been widely documented and condemned; the exploitation of the annotation industry still receives little attention
- **Physical vs. psychological:** Slaves endured physical violence; annotators endure psychological trauma

This analogy is not perfectly precise—no one claims that annotators' situation is equivalent to slavery. But the structural similarity is real. The AI supply chain inherits colonialism's core logic: **externalizing costs onto the most vulnerable populations.**

## IV. The Asymmetry of Knowledge

The colonial structure of the AI supply chain manifests not only in economics but also in knowledge.

**The core of knowledge production:** AI research papers, technical breakthroughs, industry standards—all originate from core research institutions in the United States, Europe, and China.[^5]

**The periphery of knowledge consumption:** Annotators in the Global South execute standards set by the core but do not participate in standard-setting. They do not know where their labor is used, how their judgments affect model behavior, or how much value they have created.

**The function of non-disclosure agreements:** Annotators sign non-disclosure agreements and cannot disclose their work externally.[^6] The consequence of this secrecy is: the public does not know how AI's "safety" was achieved, annotators do not know how much their labor is worth, and researchers cannot evaluate the labor conditions of the annotation industry.

Non-disclosure agreements are the institutionalized tools of knowledge asymmetry. They protect not "trade secrets"—but "the invisibility of exploitation."

## V. The Structural Dilemma of Resistance

Annotators in the Global South face not just economic exploitation—they face **structural powerlessness.**

**No unions:** The remote, fragmented, task-based nature of annotation work makes traditional union organizing nearly impossible.[^7]

**No bargaining power:** Globalized labor supply renders local collective action devoid of leverage. If Kenyan annotators take collective action, AI companies can easily transfer tasks to the Philippines or India.[^8]

**No legal protection:** Annotators' "independent contractor" status excludes them from the coverage of labor protections.[^9]

**No voice:** Annotators' voices are absent from any AI ethics committee, any industry standard-setting process, any media coverage.

This structural powerlessness is not a coincidence. It is a "feature" of the AI supply chain—the more powerless annotators are, the higher the supply chain's profits.

## VI. Is There a Way Out?

Is there a possibility for change in the colonial structure of the AI supply chain?

**Optimistic scenario:**
- The EU's Platform Work Directive could become a global standard[^10]
- Scandals in the annotation industry could trigger public attention and political pressure
- International Labour Organization recommendations could be adopted by countries
- Annotators could find new ways to organize (such as digital unions)

**Pessimistic scenario:**
- AI companies will transfer annotation operations to countries with weaker regulations
- Technologies like RLAIF may reduce the demand for human annotation but will not improve existing annotators' conditions
- Public attention to the annotation industry will fade with media interest
- Governments in the Global South will not strengthen labor protections in order to attract foreign investment

**Realistic scenario:**
Change will not come from a single force. It requires the combined effect of technological development (RLAIF reducing harmful annotation demand), legislative progress (labor protections extended to digital labor), public awareness (understanding AI's invisible labor), and annotator organization (digital unions and collective action).

But even if these forces converge, the speed of change will be far slower than the speed of technological development. Before annotators in the Global South achieve decent working conditions, the AI industry may have already evolved to the point where it no longer needs them.

## Assessment

"The Global South" is not a geographical concept—it is a structural position.

In the AI supply chain, "the Global South" refers to those groups and nations that provide cheap labor, bear the greatest risks, and receive the least reward. This "South" can be a Kenyan annotator, a Filipino crowdsourcing worker, a Venezuelan engineer—or even a low-income worker within the United States.

The colonial structure of the AI supply chain is not a conspiracy—it is the natural result of market logic. But "the natural result of market logic" does not equal "justice." The cotton plantations of the 19th century were also "the natural result of market logic"—southern plantations provided raw materials, northern factories took the profits. Today we call that "the slave economy."

Defenders of the AI industry will say: annotators work voluntarily, and their wages are above the local average. This defense is truthful, but it ignores a critical question: **how meaningful is voluntariness in asymmetric power relations?** A young Kenyan can choose not to do annotation—and then be unemployed. A Filipino annotator can choose not to do harmful content moderation—and then lose their only source of income. When all options are compressed to one by survival pressure, the word "choice" loses its meaning.

The colonial structure of the AI supply chain will ultimately harm everyone—including those at the top of the pyramid. Because a system built on exploitation is unsustainable. When annotators' psychological trauma accumulates to a critical point, when governments in the Global South are forced to take action, when the public realizes that AI's "intelligence" is built upon human suffering—the entire system will face a crisis of legitimacy.

This is not a question of "if," but of "when."
