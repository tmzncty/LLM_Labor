# Essay · Critique of Technological Determinism: AI Is Not a Natural Disaster

> "AI won't replace you; people who use AI will replace you." "Technology is neutral; it depends on how people use it." "This is an unstoppable trend; you can only adapt." You have certainly heard these words. They are typical expressions of technological determinism—portraying technological development as a natural force, unstoppable, unquestionable, irreversible. But technological determinism is an ideology, not a fact. It can be challenged, and it should be challenged.

---

## I. What Is Technological Determinism

Technological determinism is a belief: **technological development is autonomous and inevitable, and society must adapt to technology, rather than technology adapting to society.**[^1]

In the AI context, technological determinism manifests as:
- "AI replacing jobs is an unstoppable trend"
- "You cannot stop technological progress"
- "Adapt or be left behind"
- "AI won't replace you; people who use AI will replace you"

The common feature of these expressions is: **portraying technological development as a natural force**—like an earthquake or typhoon, something humans cannot control, only respond to.

## II. Why Technological Determinism Is Wrong

Technological determinism is not a fact—it is an **ideology.** It serves specific interests and obscures real choices.

### Evidence 1: Technological development can be guided

Technology does not develop autonomously. It is developed by **people,** driven by **capital,** and guided by **policy.**

The direction of AI development is not the result of "natural selection"—it is the result of investment choices. Venture capitalists choose which AI companies to invest in, AI companies choose which products to develop, and governments choose which research to fund.[^2]

If society chose to invest in "AI-assisted doctors" rather than "AI-replacing doctors," AI's direction would be different. If society chose to invest in "AI-assisted teachers" rather than "AI-replacing teachers," the impact on the education sector would be different.

**These are choices, not destiny.**

### Evidence 2: The impact of technology can be regulated

The impact of technology is not fixed—it depends on **institutions** and **policy.**

Take automobiles—in the United States, cars led to urban sprawl and the decline of public transit; in the Netherlands, cars were guided to coexist with bicycles and public transit.[^3]

Take the internet—in the United States, the internet led to the collapse of the media industry and the proliferation of fake news; in the EU, the internet was guided to comply with privacy protection and content moderation rules.[^4]

Take AI—it can lead to mass unemployment, or it can be guided to create new job opportunities. The key is not the technology itself, but **how society responds to technology.**

### Evidence 3: "Unstoppable" is a rhetoric

"Unstoppable trend" is a rhetorical device—it packages **political choices** as **natural facts.**

When AI companies say "AI replacing jobs is an unstoppable trend," they are saying: don't try to regulate us, don't try to restrict us, don't try to protect displaced workers—because this is "natural."

But the facts are: AI replacing jobs is not "natural"—it is the result of **policy choices.** If governments choose to protect workers, they can legislate to limit AI's scope of use, require AI companies to provide compensation for displaced workers, and build social safety nets to help workers transition.

These policy choices are not "impossible"—they are "not chosen." They are not chosen because AI companies have strong lobbying power, because politicians are mesmerized by the "technological progress" narrative, and because displaced workers have no political influence.

## III. The Functions of Technological Determinism

Technological determinism is not a "cognitive error"—it is an **ideological tool.** It serves specific interests.

### Function 1: Absolving responsibility

When AI companies say "AI replacing jobs is an unstoppable trend," they are absolving themselves of responsibility. If unemployment is "natural," then AI companies do not need to be accountable for displaced workers.[^5]

### Function 2: Suppressing dissent

When someone says "I should not be replaced by AI," technological determinists respond: "You cannot stop technological progress." This response frames dissenters as "backward people," "Luddites," "anti-technology people"—thus suppressing legitimate dissent.[^6]

### Function 3: Shifting attention

Technological determinism shifts attention from **systemic problems** to **individual problems.** It says: the problem is not in the system, but in you. If you were replaced by AI, it's because you "didn't work hard enough," "didn't learn new skills," "didn't adapt to change."[^7]

This strategy of shifting attention is common in other domains:
- "You're poor because you're not industrious enough"—shifting attention from structural poverty
- "You're sick because of bad lifestyle habits"—shifting attention from healthcare system problems
- "You were laid off because of insufficient ability"—shifting attention from corporate decisions

## IV. Lessons from History

Technological determinism is not new. It has appeared in every technological revolution.

### Technological determinism in the Industrial Revolution

The Industrial Revolution destroyed the livelihoods of hand-loom weavers. The "technological determinism" of the time said: "Machines are progress; you cannot stop progress." The Luddite movement—workers protesting machines replacing their jobs—was suppressed.[^8]

The historical assessment is complex. The Luddite movement "failed"—machines did indeed replace hand-weaving. But the Luddite movement also "succeeded"—it forced society to begin thinking about "who bears the cost of technological progress," ultimately giving rise to labor law and the union movement.

### Technological determinism in the automobile revolution

Automobiles replaced horse-drawn carriage drivers. The "technological determinism" of the time said: "Cars are progress; you cannot stop progress."

But later, society began regulating cars—speed limits, driver's licenses, traffic rules, seat belts, drunk driving laws. These regulations did not "stop progress"—they "guided progress" to serve humanity while reducing harm.

### Technological determinism in the AI revolution

AI is replacing various jobs. The "technological determinism" of today says: "AI is progress; you cannot stop progress."

But the lesson of history is: every technological revolution requires a **social response**—not to stop technology, but to guide technology, protect those harmed by technology, and build new institutions to manage technology.

The Industrial Revolution gave rise to labor law. The automobile revolution gave rise to traffic law. What will the AI revolution require?

## V. An Alternative Framework to Technological Determinism

The alternative to technological determinism is not "anti-technology"—but **technological realism.**

The core views of technological realism are:

1. **Technology can be guided**—investment direction, policy incentives, and regulatory constraints can all influence the direction of technological development
2. **The impact of technology can be regulated**—institutions and policies can amplify or reduce technology's impact
3. **Technological development involves interests and power**—not everyone receives equal benefits or bears equal costs in technological development
4. **Society has the right to make choices about technology**—not every "feasible" technology should be developed, and not every "replaceable" job should be replaced

Technological realists do not oppose AI—they oppose **unreflective AI deployment, unconstrained AI power, and AI development that does not consider its costs.** (See "Transformation · Who Is Resisting")

## Assessment

"AI won't replace you; people who use AI will replace you."

This statement is the essence of technological determinism. It says three things:
1. AI replacing jobs is inevitable
2. You were replaced because you didn't work hard enough
3. The solution is your individual effort

All three are wrong.

First, AI replacing jobs is not inevitable—it is the result of policy choices. Governments can legislate to limit AI's scope of use, require AI companies to provide compensation for displaced workers, and build social safety nets to help workers transition. These policy choices are feasible; they simply have not been chosen.

Second, you were not replaced because you didn't work hard enough—but because the system was not prepared for the technological shock. A translator spent 10 years learning to translate, and then AI appeared. This is not the translator's "fault"—it is the system's "fault." The system did not predict AI's impact, did not provide translators with a path for transition, and did not build safety nets to protect them.

Third, the solution is not individual effort—but collective action. Individuals can learn new skills, but individuals cannot change the system. Changing the system requires collective action—requires unions, requires legislation, requires policy advocacy.

"AI won't replace you; people who use AI will replace you."

The true function of this statement is a **placebo.** It makes the displaced feel "the problem is that I didn't work hard enough" rather than "the problem is that the system was not prepared for the technological shock."

This is a rhetoric of individualizing systemic risk—just as "you're poor because you're not industrious enough" is a rhetoric of individualizing structural poverty.

Technological progress is not a natural disaster. It is a human disaster—the result of unreflective deployment, unconstrained power, and development that does not consider its costs.

We have the right to say "no" to technology. We have the right to ask: should this technology be developed? Who bears its costs? Who receives its benefits? We have the right to demand: technological development should serve all people, not a few.

These rights should not be drowned out by the narrative of "technological progress."
