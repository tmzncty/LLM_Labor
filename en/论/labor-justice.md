# Labor Justice: Distributing Gains, Risks and Decision Rights

Labor justice in AI is not a slogan that “AI is built on suffering.” It asks at least three distinct questions: how gains are distributed, who bears risk, and whether workers participate in decisions about technological deployment.

The ILO's 2025 global exposure index estimates that about one in four workers is in an occupation with some GenAI exposure, while transformation is more likely than whole-job replacement for most occupations.[^1] The justice question is therefore not simply whether automation happens, but whether productivity gains become wages, shorter hours and safer work—or only cost reduction.

Moderation, annotation and model evaluation can shift psychological and income risk onto workers deep in supply chains. OECD evidence on algorithmic management also identifies accountability, explainability and worker-health concerns, while worker consultation can improve outcomes.[^2]

Collective bargaining adds a procedural dimension: notice, consent, compensation and the right to negotiate AI use.

Justice is a normative framework, not a directly measured statistic. Evidence can show unequal burdens or weak participation; it cannot make an entire industry morally uniform from a single case.
