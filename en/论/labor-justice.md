# Essay · Labor Justice: The Invisible Labor in the Age of AI

> When you ask ChatGPT a question and it gives a safe, appropriate, and helpful answer, that answer's "safety" is not a product of mathematics—it was annotated item by item by a Kenyan worker earning less than two dollars per hour, reading the darkest texts of human civilization. AI's "intelligence" is built upon massive quantities of low-wage, high-psychological-damage, invisible human labor. This is not a technical problem—it is a labor justice problem. And the entire ingenuity of this industry is dedicated to ensuring you never think about it.

---

Labor justice is not an abstract concept. Its core question is specific: **who bears the cost of a given form of labor, and who reaps the benefits—and are the two symmetrical?** When a factory worker's finger is severed by a machine while the factory owner profits—that is a labor justice problem. When a coal miner develops pneumoconiosis while the mining company pays taxes on its earnings—that is a labor justice problem. When an annotator reads texts describing child abuse to train AI "safety," while the AI company achieves a valuation of hundreds of billions of dollars—**that too is a labor justice problem.**

The mainstream narrative of the AI industry sidesteps this question. It speaks of "model capabilities," "alignment techniques," "emergent behaviors"—these terms direct attention to the technology itself rather than the labor behind it. This essay's task is to pull that attention back: not asking "what can AI do," but asking "whose labor is AI's capability built upon, and is that labor treated fairly?" This question requires no technical background to understand—it requires only a basic moral intuition: **the cost a person bears should be commensurate with the reward they receive.**

Compared to "Data Annotation and AI Labor" in LLM_Chronicle, this essay takes a different angle. That work is investigative narrative—using facts to expose the face of the industry; this work is ethical inquiry—using frameworks of justice to analyze the moral implications of those facts. Facts can be shared, but judgments must be independent. This essay assumes the reader is already familiar with the basic facts of the annotation industry (if not, it is recommended to first read "Investigation · Kenyan Labelers" and "Investigation · The Psychological Cost of Data Annotation") and proceeds directly to the inquiry of justice.

## I. The Human Cost of RLHF: Who Pays the Price of Safety

RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) is the core technique for making large models "obedient." In 2022, InstructGPT established this paradigm: collecting human annotators' preference rankings of model outputs, training a reward model, then using reinforcement learning to adjust the language model.[^1] This pipeline is technically elegant—three steps, clean and clear. But it conceals an ugly fact: **RLHF's "human feedback" comes from the most vulnerable workforce in the world.**

In January 2023, Billy Perrigo of *TIME* published an investigative report exposing OpenAI's outsourced annotation operations in Kenya. OpenAI hired annotators through Sama (a San Francisco-based data annotation company with offices in Kenya), paying between $1.32 and $2 per hour.[^2] The annotators' job was to read harmful texts that ChatGPT might generate—descriptions of child sexual abuse, genocide, torture, suicide methods—and then label the "degree of harm" of this content, used to train the model to refuse such requests.

**ChatGPT's "safety" was purchased with these people's mental health.**

These annotators were not engineers, not researchers, not even long-term employees. Most were temporary workers hired through Sama, on short-term contracts, without mental health support, without long-term medical coverage. During eight-hour shifts, they read the darkest texts in human civilization—passages describing child violence, detailed suicide instructions, texts glorifying genocide—and then went home. Reports indicate that multiple annotators developed symptoms of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD): insomnia, nightmares, emotional numbness, increased fear in daily life.[^2]

Sama terminated its contract with OpenAI in February 2022—partly because the impact of annotation work on employees' mental health had raised internal concerns. But OpenAI's annotation needs did not disappear; they simply shifted to other outsourcing companies and other countries. The operational logic of the entire annotation industry is: **if workers in one place start complaining or attract media attention, move to another, quieter place.**

The $1.32-$2/hour wage in Kenya is not the lowest—the local minimum wage is approximately $1.15/hour. But this comparison itself is a form of obfuscation: annotators read the darkest content in human civilization, enduring work environments that psychology has documented as traumatic—what should the "market price" for this work be? $2? $20? $200? The answer depends on how you price human mental health. And the market's answer is: **as cheaply as possible.**

There exists here a precise inequality. The model's safety level is positively correlated with the annotators' psychological trauma: the more harmful content annotators see, the more detailed their annotations, the "safer" the model becomes. But the more harmful content annotators see, the more their mental health deteriorates. Safety and harm are not exchanged between the model and the user—they are exchanged across the bodies of annotators. Who gets safety? All users. Who gets harm? A handful of Kenyans. **This exchange was never made with their consent.**

This is not just an OpenAI problem. Nearly every company that uses RLHF—Google, Anthropic, Meta—relies on similar outsourced annotation systems. The nature of annotation work is the commodification of human "judgment": you need a person to determine "whether this answer is harmful," but you do not want to pay a high salary for that judgment. So annotation is outsourced to places with the lowest labor costs—Kenya, the Philippines, India, Venezuela—done by those with the least bargaining power.

This "commodification of judgment" has a key characteristic: it does not require mechanical labor but rather capacities unique to humans—moral intuition, cultural sensitivity, boundary judgment. AI models require human annotation precisely because these capacities are what current AI lacks most. The irony is: the annotation industry hires humans to do what AI cannot (moral judgment), then uses the results of that annotation to train AI to no longer need humans to do these things. **Annotators are teaching AI how to replace themselves—but they have not been told this.**

It is instructive to compare the treatment of hazardous work in other industries. Coal miners face the risk of pneumoconiosis, but their wages are at least above the local average; deep-sea fishermen face mortal danger, but their income is at the top of the fishing industry; chemical plant workers are exposed to toxic substances, but they have protective equipment, regular health checks, and hazard pay. Why? Because society—at least nominally—acknowledges that "high risk should be compensated with high pay." But the annotators' situation breaks this logic: they face high risk and low compensation. The reason is simple—their labor is invisible. A person in the suburbs of Nairobi reading harmful texts will not appear in any company's annual report. A person in a Manila apartment reviewing hate speech will not be mentioned in any AI ethics paper. **Invisibility is the best disguise for injustice.**

## II. The Global Annotation Supply Chain: A Pyramid of Profit, an Inverted Pyramid of Risk

RLHF is just the tip of the iceberg. The entire large model industry—from pre-training to fine-tuning to evaluation—is built upon a massive global data annotation supply chain.

**Scale AI** is the largest company in this industry. Headquartered in San Francisco, founded by Alexandr Wang in 2016, valued at $14 billion in 2024.[^3] Scale AI does not directly employ annotators—it operates a platform (Remotasks being its brand for individual annotators) connecting "AI companies that need annotated data" with "workers willing to do annotation work." This model is like Uber for drivers—the platform does not employ workers, only provides matching services; workers operate as independent contractors, without minimum wage guarantees, benefits, or unions.[^4]

Annotators can be categorized into three tiers by work type, each with different labor intensity, skill requirements, and compensation:

- **Data annotation** (image classification, text classification, semantic segmentation): lowest skill requirements, lowest unit price, most easily replaced by AI. A typical task is drawing boxes around objects in images—"this is a pedestrian," "this is a traffic light." This type of annotation is the foundation of autonomous driving training data and the most thoroughly commodified labor in the annotation industry.
- **Content moderation** (harmful content identification, hate speech detection, suicide risk assessment): highest psychological risk, annotators bear the greatest trauma. Their daily work involves reading the darkest posts on social media, determining whether content constitutes hate speech, evaluating whether an image involves violence. The PTSD problems of Facebook/Meta's content moderators were systematically exposed by Casey Newton of The Verge as early as 2019.[^14] The AI industry's harmful content moderation is essentially a variant of the same type of labor.
- **RLHF preference annotation** (evaluating AI answer quality, ranking preferences, assessing safety): highest skill requirements, slightly higher unit prices—but still "high harm, low compensation." RLHF annotators need to understand subtle linguistic differences, judge the safety boundaries of answers, and select the "relatively less bad" option between two "not very good" choices. This is a highly specialized form of judgment—but the market still prices it near minimum wage.

Profit distribution is a precise pyramid. A typical annotation task chain: **AI company (e.g., OpenAI) → annotation platform (e.g., Scale AI) → secondary outsourcing → annotator**. The fees AI companies pay for annotated data are mostly captured by the platform and middlemen; annotators receive a pittance at the bottom. On Remotasks, Filipino annotators reportedly earn $1-$3 per hour; in India and Venezuela, some simple annotation tasks pay as low as $0.10 per task.[^4] In extreme cases, annotators' hourly wages fall below $1.[^15]

Annotation platforms typically price by "per task" rather than "per hour"—annotators' actual hourly earnings depend on their speed, and platforms can control costs by adjusting task difficulty and unit prices. When annotator supply is abundant, unit prices decrease. **If you won't do it, someone else will.** This is identical to the situation of Uber drivers—the platform holds all pricing power, while workers hold zero bargaining power.

Appen (an Australian public company, once the largest AI data annotation company) saw its stock price fall more than 95% from its 2020 peak by 2024.[^6] Appen's decline illustrates a deeper truth: the annotation industry is itself a consumable of AI—when models no longer require a certain type of annotation, the company doing that annotation will be eliminated. **The eliminated company is a loss of capital; the eliminated annotator is a loss of livelihood—but in news reports, these two losses are placed in the same sentence as if they were equivalent.**

The structural injustice of this supply chain is not in "outsourcing" itself—outsourcing is a legitimate business model. The injustice lies in: **the direction of profit distribution and the direction of risk distribution are completely opposite.** AI companies take the profit and reputation, annotation platforms take the intermediary fees, annotators take—less than the price of a cup of coffee per hour—along with PTSD.

The global annotation supply chain also exhibits a **structural characteristic of neocolonialism**: core countries (the United States, Europe) define the standards, set the rules, and take the profits; peripheral countries (Kenya, the Philippines, India, Venezuela) provide the labor, bear the risks, and take the scraps. This is not a conspiracy—it is the natural result of market logic. But "the natural result of market logic" does not equal "justice." The cotton plantations of the 19th century were also "the natural result of market logic"—southern plantations provided raw materials, northern factories took the profits. Today we call that "the slave economy." The structure of the annotation industry is certainly not equivalent to slavery—but it inherits the same inverted logic of profit and risk, differing only in degree. **The question is not "whether it is equally severe," but "whether this direction should be accepted."**

A neglected fact is: annotators are not only "exploited workers"—they are also "victims of skill transfer" in the Global South of the AI industry. Annotation platforms' promotional rhetoric is "helping people in developing countries acquire digital skills." But in reality, the skills of annotation work—drawing boxes, labeling, sorting—are not "digital skills" but "repetitive mechanical labor." Annotators do not accumulate human capital, do not acquire transferable technical capabilities, do not build professional networks. They consume time in exchange for meager income—and when tasks are replaced by AI, they return to square one.

More disturbingly, annotation platforms often attract annotators under the guise of "training"—"join us, learn AI technology, advance your career skills." But the so-called "training" is nothing more than teaching you how to use annotation tools. These tools—box-drawing software, labeling systems, ranking interfaces—are useless outside of annotation. **"Training" is not helping annotators grow; it is reducing the platform's management costs.** A "trained" annotator works faster, makes fewer errors, and generates more profit for the platform—but the annotator's own market value has not increased by a single cent. This is the same logic as 19th-century factory owners teaching workers how to operate machines more efficiently—the beneficiary of "training" is capital, not labor.

## III. From RLHF to RLAIF to Pure RL: Will Annotators Be Liberated or Abandoned

The demand for human annotation in large models is undergoing a clear trajectory of decline.

**RLHF** (2022): fully dependent on human annotation. Human annotators rank model outputs by preference, training a reward model. The quality of human annotation directly determines model safety.[^1]

**RLAIF** (2022-2023): partially replacing human annotation. Anthropic proposed Constitutional AI—using a set of "constitutional" principles (behavioral guidelines) for the model to self-criticize and self-correct, reducing reliance on manual annotation. Humans still need to draft the constitution, but no longer need to annotate item by item.[^7]

**Pure RL** (2025): DeepSeek-R1-Zero used pure reinforcement learning with rule-based rewards, developing reasoning capabilities without any human-annotated data.[^8] R1-Zero's chain of thought emerged from scratch—it was not taught "what good reasoning is" but discovered good reasoning patterns through trial and error.

These three steps constitute a clear trajectory: from complete dependence on human judgment, to partial dependence, to potential complete independence. The technical literature describes this as "reducing reliance on human annotation"—that is the technical narrative. From the perspective of labor justice, this trajectory has a different name: **systematic deskilling and abandonment.**

"Deskilling" is a classic concept in labor studies: when a technology can replace a worker's particular skill, that worker loses the market value of that skill. What RLAIF replaces is not just the action of "annotation"—it replaces the capacity of "human judgment." When an annotator spends two years learning how to precisely assess the harmfulness of AI outputs, the emergence of RLAIF renders that skill worthless. This is not the normal cycle of "technological progress rendering old skills obsolete"—because annotators have never received rewards commensurate with the costs they bore. The skills they purchased with their mental health were declared obsolete while they were still suffering from PTSD.

There is a neglected historical lesson here. Every technological revolution has been accompanied by "deskilling"—the Industrial Revolution turned artisans into assembly line workers, automation turned assembly line workers into machine attendants. But history also shows: after each deskilling, new skill demands emerge, and new workers fill new positions. **The question is the "transition period."** The Industrial Revolution's "transition period" lasted decades, during which millions of workers fell into destitution. How long will the AI revolution's "transition period" last? Can annotators and other displaced workers survive this transition? Who will pay for their survival during it?

If this trend continues, what is the outlook for annotators?

The optimistic narrative is "liberation": humans will no longer need to do harmful content moderation, no longer need to train models while suffering PTSD, AI can self-evolve, and annotators can move on to more meaningful work. This narrative is beautiful—but it ignores a basic fact: **annotators stop annotating, then what?** Annotators are not people who "chose" to annotate—annotation was the least-bad option available to them. What RLAIF eliminates is not their "choice" but their "least-bad option."

The pessimistic narrative is "abandonment": tens of thousands of Kenyan, Filipino, and Indian workers who depend on annotation for their livelihood will be cast into the flood of unemployment, just like factory workers replaced by automation. The AI industry's response will most likely be silence—just as automated manufacturing never answered "what happens to workers replaced by robots."

But there is a third possibility: **AI will not fully replace human annotation but will push annotators into more refined, more hidden, more unprotected positions.** When simple annotation is replaced by RLAIF, the remaining annotation work will be of the highest difficulty—judging subtle cultural biases, evaluating complex ethical boundaries, handling edge cases that AI cannot independently assess. These jobs require higher skills, but compensation may not increase—because the logic of "there are still people willing to do it" still holds. Moreover, as the total number of annotators decreases, their collective voice becomes weaker—an annotator community of only a few hundred people finds it far harder to organize collective action than one with tens of thousands. **RLAIF may not "liberate" annotators but rather push them from "large-scale exploitation" to "small-scale abandonment."**

DeepSeek-R1-Zero raises a profound question: when models no longer need human annotation, does annotator labor still have value? From a technical perspective, the answer may be "no." But from a justice perspective, the answer is entirely different—**a person's labor value should not be determined by "whether the market still needs you."** Annotators have paid a real psychological cost for AI's growth, and these costs do not disappear simply because the market no longer needs annotation. A society that acknowledges your value only when it needs you, and abandons you when it does not—this is not efficiency, this is injustice.

There is an analogy worth tracing. In 19th-century America, cotton picking was an industry highly dependent on human labor. When mechanical picking emerged, millions of sharecroppers who depended on picking for their livelihood lost their only source of income. Society did not ask "these people gave generations of labor to the cotton industry; when they are no longer needed, what should they receive?" Society said "the market no longer needs you." **The situation annotators face today is structurally isomorphic.** The annotation industry will not ask "what contributions did annotators make to AI"—it will only ask "are annotators still useful now?" When the answer is "no," annotators will be scrapped like old machines.

Of course, this analogy is not perfectly precise—19th-century sharecroppers had no choice, while 21st-century annotators at least nominally have a choice. But "nominal choice" is almost meaningless in the face of survival pressure. A young Kenyan can choose not to do annotation—and then be unemployed. A Filipino annotator can choose not to do harmful content moderation—and then lose their only source of income. **When all options are compressed to one by survival pressure, the word "choice" loses its meaning.**

## IV. Workers Replaced by AI: Unemployment That Is Already Happening

"Will AI replace human jobs?"—the tense of this question is wrong. The correct tense is not "will" but "is."

**Klarna** (the Swedish fintech company) announced in 2024 that its AI customer service assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month online, equivalent to the workload of 700 full-time customer service representatives.[^9] Klarna subsequently froze hiring for human customer service. These 700 representatives—they are not a number, they are 700 people with families, rent, and children's tuition. Klarna's stock price rose due to AI efficiency gains—700 people's unemployment was recorded as "cost optimization" on the financial statements. **BT** (British Telecom) announced in 2023 plans to cut up to 55,000 jobs over 5-7 years.[^10] **IBM** CEO Arvind Krishna stated in 2023 that approximately 7,800 back-office positions could be replaced by AI.[^11] Three companies, three continents, three different industries—the same trend: AI replacing human labor, profits going to companies, costs going to individuals.

In late 2024, the San Francisco AI company **Artisan** put up billboards reading "Stop Hiring Humans."[^12] Artisan sells AI sales representatives, and its marketing message is: your sales team can be replaced by an AI Agent that needs no salary, no vacation, and will never quit. "Stop Hiring Humans" caused controversy not because it said something new—but because it loudly stated what the industry was thinking privately. AI company CEOs discuss in closed-door meetings "how to use AI to replace 80% of customer service teams," but in public say "AI won't replace you; people who use AI will replace you." Artisan's ad tore away this veneer of respectability.

Those being replaced are not just "low-skill" positions. The affected occupations are contracting from both ends toward the middle:

**Translation.** DeepL and GPT-4's translation quality has approached that of human translators for common language pairs. For "good enough" translation needs—business emails, product descriptions, news summaries—AI is already competent. Only in literary translation, legal translation, medical translation, and other scenarios requiring deep domain knowledge are human translators still irreplaceable. But "good enough" translation accounts for the majority of the translation market's total volume—when this portion of demand is met by AI, large numbers of mid- and low-tier translators lose their market. A freelance translator faces a situation where: rates are falling ever lower because clients know AI can do "eighty percent," and the premium for the "twenty percent" you provide is being compressed. When that premium is compressed to near zero, the translator is no longer "a professional enhanced by AI"—but "slightly better than AI yet far more expensive luxury."

**Illustration.** Midjourney and DALL-E's commercial applications have already caused a sharp decline in orders for freelance illustrators. Publishers in Japan and South Korea have begun using AI to generate light novel covers and chapter illustrations. In 2023, a children's book with AI-generated illustrations was listed on Amazon, sparking widespread protest from the illustration community.[^17] Did the protests have an effect? Yes—they let some readers know the cover illustration was AI-generated. But did the orders come back? No. An illustrator who spent ten years honing their style, and AI can imitate it in seconds—this is not competition, this is a dimensional reduction attack. Illustrators' only remaining barriers are "attribution rights" and "emotional connection"—but the market's willingness to pay for these two things is rapidly declining.

**Junior programming.** GitHub Copilot and Cursor can already automatically generate large amounts of basic code—function implementations, unit tests, API integrations, bug fixes. A senior engineer using Copilot can accomplish the work that previously required two junior engineers. (See the "AI Programming Assistants" chronicle.) Companies' demand for junior programmers is declining—not because junior programmers are "bad," but because an AI + senior engineer combination is cheaper, faster, and more reliable.

This creates a paradox. Junior programmers need to do junior-level work to gain experience and grow into senior engineers. But when junior-level work is replaced by AI, junior programmers lose the pathway to gaining experience. **If no one gives junior programmers an opportunity, there will be no new senior engineers—so ten years from now, when today's batch of senior engineers retires, who will "use AI"?** This is the long-term paradox of AI replacing labor: short-term efficiency gains, long-term talent gaps. Companies do not care about this problem—because the talent gap ten years from now is not on this quarter's earnings report.

**Customer service.** Not just Klarna. Nearly every consumer-facing company is deploying AI customer service. Human customer service representatives are being reduced to a "fallback for complex problems that AI cannot handle"—and AI's capability boundary is continuously expanding, shrinking the space left for that "fallback." A typical customer service team change: 20 human representatives in 2022, reduced to 8 after deploying AI in 2023, further reduced to 5 in 2024. Each person is told "your job is to handle what AI cannot"—but no one is told that when the problems AI cannot handle become fewer, your position will also become fewer.

The key characteristic of this replacement pattern is: **it is not "a position fully replaced by AI" but "a position's staffing requirements dramatically reduced by AI."** A team that originally needed 10 customer service representatives now needs 3 (plus AI). A team that originally needed 5 junior programmers now needs 2 (plus Copilot). Each reduction seems modest—30%, 50%—but when this reduction occurs simultaneously across all industries and all countries, the total employment impact is enormous.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei predicted in 2025: AI could eliminate up to 50% of entry-level white-collar positions within 1-5 years.[^13] This is not the alarmism of AI critics—it is the self-warning of the AI industry's most important CEO. When an AI company's CEO tells you AI may replace your job, continuing to say "AI won't replace you" is a form of deception.

"AI won't replace you; people who use AI will replace you"—is this statement truth or placebo? Three rounds of reasoning:

**First round**: It describes a real short-term trend—people who master AI tools are indeed gaining competitive advantage. A programmer who can use Copilot is more productive than one who cannot. This is a fact.
**Second round**: But it conceals a structural problem—the threshold for "being able to use AI" is getting lower and lower. When everyone "can use AI," the competitive advantage disappears, and all that remains is "being replaced by AI." More critically—"being able to use AI" is itself being automated by AI. When Agents can operate autonomously, the gap between "people who can use AI" and "people who cannot use AI" vanishes.
**Third round**: The deeper problem is—even if you "can use AI," your value is anchored to AI's capabilities. You are not an independent worker; you are an appendage of AI. **Your salary is not because you have value, but because you can make AI deliver value.** When AI no longer needs you to "deliver"—for example, when AI can operate autonomously—your value drops to zero.

The true function of this statement is placebo: it makes the displaced feel "the problem is that I didn't work hard enough" rather than "the problem is that the system was not prepared for the technological shock." This is a rhetoric of individualizing systemic risk—just as "you're poor because you're not industrious enough" is a rhetoric of individualizing structural poverty.

## V. Invisible Harm: The Psychological Cost of Annotators

The psychological harm of annotation work is a well-documented but rarely seriously addressed issue.

In 2019, Casey Newton of The Verge published an in-depth investigation of Facebook/Meta content moderators.[^14] Moderators were exposed to large volumes of violence, hate, and pornographic content during their shifts, with PTSD rates far exceeding the general population. Multiple moderators reported nightmares, emotional numbness, social withdrawal, and conditioned fear of specific stimuli (such as balloons, specific colors). Some moderators continued to exhibit trauma symptoms years after leaving their positions. A former moderator told Newton: "I now feel nauseated when I see anything red—because red reminds me of the images I saw during moderation."

The AI industry's harmful content annotation is essentially a variant of the same type of labor—but more hidden than social media moderation. Facebook's moderators at least have legal protections in the United States (although limited), while AI industry annotators in Kenya and the Philippines face weaker legal protections, less media attention, and collective action that is nearly impossible.

A key principle of labor justice: **the compensation standard for hazardous working environments should not be determined by labor market supply and demand, but by the actual severity of the work's hazards.** Mine workers receive hazard pay not because "the market thinks they're worth that much" but because "society acknowledges they bear risks that should not be priced at minimum wage." The "hazardous substance" annotators face is psychological trauma—but the entire industry's pricing logic is "they have no other choice, so $2 is enough."

A notable phenomenon: annotators' trauma symptoms are often not recognized. When a miner develops pneumoconiosis, it is treated as an occupational injury—with diagnosis, compensation, and legal protection. When an annotator develops PTSD, it is treated as "a personal psychological problem"—without diagnosis, without compensation, without a legal framework to recognize "psychological trauma from annotating harmful content" as an occupational injury. **Labor law has not yet established a protective framework for hazardous work in the digital age—this is not the annotators' failure, but a failure of institutions.**

## VI. Is Collective Action Possible for Workers

The traditional path to achieving labor justice is collective action—unions, strikes, collective bargaining. This path is almost entirely absent in the annotation industry.

Three reasons. First, annotators' status is "independent contractor" rather than "employee"—collective bargaining rights in labor law typically apply only to employment relationships. Annotation platforms use the label of "flexible employment" to exclude annotators from the coverage of labor protections. Second, the remote, fragmented, task-based nature of annotation work makes traditional union organizing nearly impossible—annotators are not in the same factory, do not know each other, and do not share physical space. Their "workplace" is their own bedroom; their "colleagues" are anonymous usernames on the platform. Third, the supply of annotators is global—if Kenyan annotators take collective action, AI companies can easily transfer tasks to the Philippines or India. Globalized labor supply renders local collective action devoid of leverage. Workers in one place have awakened—no matter, switch to a place that hasn't.

This does not mean annotators have not resisted. After the 2023 TIME report, some Kenyan annotators began speaking out through international media.[^2] In 2024, Filipino Remotasks annotators shared details of working conditions on social media, drawing a small amount of attention. But these actions were scattered, individualized—they did not form institutional collective power. **The annotation industry's structure is designed to resist collective action—this is not an accident, but a feature.** Every design decision of the platform economy—remote work, per-task payment, independent contractor status, global supply—is simultaneously an optimization for efficiency and a dissolution of collective action.

A dispiriting comparison: engineers at AI companies can strike (Google employees protested the company's contract with the Israeli military in 2024), and their strike draws media attention and company response. Annotators' "strike"—stopping taking tasks—would not be noticed by anyone, because others would immediately take over their tasks. **A voice that can be heard has value—but a voice's value precisely depends on whether you have leverage that cannot be replaced.** Annotators have no leverage. Their entire leverage is "willing to do work that others are not willing to do at the lowest price"—and this leverage has zero scarcity in a globalized labor market.

This is not the annotators' fault. This is a structural fault. A system that places the most vulnerable workers in the position of least bargaining power, and then says "this is the natural result of the market"—this is not a description of structure, but a rationalization of injustice. Markets are never "natural"—markets are shaped by rules, and rules are made by those with power. When the rule-makers and beneficiaries are the same people, "the natural result of the market" is just another way of saying "the natural result of power."

## VII. Who Should Be Responsible for AI's Labor Costs

The core question of AI labor justice is not "whether AI is replacing people"—that is a factual description. The core question is: **who should be held responsible?**

**First layer: the companies developing models.** OpenAI has derived ChatGPT's safety from annotators' psychological trauma, and from safety it has gained billions of users and hundreds of billions in valuation. But OpenAI has never provided mental health compensation to Kenyan annotators, nor has it publicly acknowledged their contributions. The companies developing models are the most direct beneficiaries of labor costs—they have a responsibility to ensure that workers in their supply chain are treated fairly. But "fairness" is nearly impossible to define in the context of cross-border outsourcing: what is a "fair" hourly wage for a Kenyan annotator? By Kenyan standards? By American standards? By the compensation standard for psychological trauma? There is no consensus on this question—but no consensus does not mean no responsibility. At a minimum, annotators should receive informed consent (knowing what content they will face), mental health support, and compensation no lower than the local decent standard of living.

**Second layer: companies using APIs.** When a company replaces 700 human customer service representatives with AI customer service, that company is the executor of the layoff decision. AI companies provide tools; companies make choices—but whose responsibility is this choice? A common excuse is "we are simply using the best tools available on the market"—but when you use the "best tools" to fire 700 people, you cannot enjoy the dividends of efficiency while not bearing the consequences of unemployment. Companies should at least bear transition costs: retraining programs, severance compensation, transition support. But the reality is—most companies can barely manage "notification." When Klarna froze customer service hiring, it announced no transition plan for existing customer service staff; when IBM announced its AI replacement plan, the language was "hiring freeze" rather than "compensating existing employees"—the precise choice of language reveals the precise evasion of responsibility.

**Third layer: government regulation.** Labor law is designed on the assumption that "a person has one employer." But the reality of the annotation industry is: annotators take tasks through platforms, are independent contractors, are not protected by minimum wage, have no overtime pay, and no work injury insurance. The business model of annotation platforms essentially exploits loopholes in labor law—"we don't employ them, we only provide matching services." This is not a technical problem; it is a governance problem. The EU's AI Act has begun touching on the regulation of "high-risk AI systems," but it focuses on model output's harm to users—rather than model training's harm to annotators.[^16] This is a significant blind spot. California's AB 5 bill attempted to reclassify "independent contractors" as "employees" to expand labor protections but was met with fierce lobbying from the tech industry—Uber, Lyft, and DoorDash spent over $200 million pushing through Proposition 22 to exempt themselves. The business model of annotation platforms is identical to that of gig economy platforms—if the labor protection issues of the gig economy remain unsolved to this day, the outlook for the annotation industry is equally bleak.

There is a particularly disturbing fact here: **do annotators have the right to refuse to review harmful content?** In an ideal state, the answer should be "yes"—any worker has the right to refuse a hazardous working environment. But in reality, refusing to review harmful content means losing tasks and losing income. The annotators' "choice" is: look at harmful content and get paid, or don't look and starve. **This is not a choice; this is coercion.** In any other industry—sewage treatment, mine operations, chemical production—labor law mandates the safety protections employers must provide. The "hazardous substance" annotators face is not chemical but psychological—but labor law has not yet built a protective framework for this type of harm. When a worker has the right to refuse to operate a machine that has not passed a safety inspection, why does an annotator not have the right to refuse to review unclassified harmful content? The answer is: because their "employer" does not even acknowledge itself as an employer.

There is a massive absence in "AI ethics" discussions: **the voices of workers.** When academia discusses AI ethics, it discusses bias, fairness, transparency, accountability—these are all ethics at the model level. When policymakers discuss AI regulation, it discusses safety, copyright, privacy—these are all protections at the user level. Who is discussing annotators? Who is discussing customer service representatives and translators replaced by AI? The answer is: almost no one.

The existing framework of AI ethics is **designed around models and users**—it cares about "whether AI is fair to users," not "whether AI's production process is fair to workers." This is like discussing whether a shirt is "well-fitting, attractive, and durable" without discussing whether the workers in the factory that produced it received minimum wage. Or, more precisely—like discussing whether a building's earthquake resistance meets standards without discussing whether the construction workers who built it had safety helmets that met standards. **AI ethics that does not include labor justice is not complete ethics—it is ethics designed for those who already hold power.**

A disturbing fact: the loudest "AI ethics" advocates in the AI industry—ethics teams at major AI companies, AI ethics research centers in academia—have almost none been built around "annotators' labor rights." They discuss bias, discuss fairness, discuss existential risk—but annotators' PTSD is not on their agenda. This is not because annotators' problems are "unimportant"—but because annotators have no money to fund research, no volume to influence policy, and no seats at the table for discussion. **The agenda of ethics discussions is itself a mapping of power structures.**

## Assessment

This diviner projects three rounds.

**First round: the factual level.** AI's "intelligence" is built upon low-wage, high-psychological-damage, invisible human labor. Annotators read harmful texts to train models "safety," earning $1.32-$2/hour, receiving PTSD in return. The profit pyramid and inverted risk pyramid of the annotation industry are precisely inverted. All of this is well-documented. The referenced essay "Data Annotation and AI Labor" has recorded the facts; this essay will not repeat them—what this essay seeks to do is to inquire.

**Second round: the trend level.** RLAIF and pure RL are reducing the demand for human annotation. It is not only annotators being replaced by AI—customer service, translation, illustration, and junior programming are all experiencing position reductions. "AI won't replace you; people who use AI will replace you"—when projected to the third round, this statement is a placebo that individualizes systemic risk. Annotators are not being "liberated"—they are being "abandoned," and without transition plans.

**Third round: the justice level.** This is the core argument of this essay—and the fundamental distinction from investigative narrative.

"Data Annotation and AI Labor" answers the question "how are annotators doing." This essay asks the question "is this just." The answer is: **it is unjust.** The injustice is not in "technology replacing labor"—technology replacing labor has been the norm since the Industrial Revolution. The injustice lies in three structural misalignments:

**First, the misalignment between cost and benefit.** AI companies have gained valuations and profits, users have gained convenience and safety, and annotators have gained—trauma and being forgotten. Displaced workers have gained—"learn AI." The benefits are concentrated at the top of the pyramid (tech company shareholders, executives), while the costs sink to the bottom (annotators, displaced low-skill workers). This is not accidental—it is the inevitable product of the incentive structure: companies pursue profit maximization, and the path to profit maximization is "compressing labor costs as much as possible."

**Second, the misalignment between visibility and contribution.** OpenAI's technical papers list the names of hundreds of researchers—annotators' names are not among them. ChatGPT's launch showcased the product's polished interface—annotators' working conditions were not part of it. Annotators' contribution to AI is structural and indispensable—yet they are nonexistent in AI's narrative. This invisibility is not an information gap—it is carefully maintained. Outsourcing, remote work, per-task payment, independent contractor status—each layer adds a wall of separation between annotators and the public.

**Third, the misalignment between rights and obligations.** AI companies have an obligation to provide "safe" products to users—but no obligation to ensure that the process of producing "safety" is itself safe. Companies have the right to use AI to replace workers—but no obligation to provide transition support for displaced workers. Annotators have an obligation to complete annotation tasks—but no right to refuse harmful content. The entire system's distribution of rights and obligations is asymmetric: the powerful party (companies, platforms) bears the fewest obligations, while the powerless party (annotators, the displaced) bears the most.

These three misalignments are not independent—they form a self-reinforcing structure. The cost misalignment leads to profit concentration in a few companies, which use profits to influence policymaking, which results in further weakening of labor protections, which makes the cost misalignment even worse. This is a positive feedback loop—injustice self-proliferates within the system.

This is not a question of "whether AI will replace people." AI is already replacing people. The question is: whether we choose to see or not to see.

Labor justice demands not just "better AI"—it demands that we ask three questions:

**Who is paying the price for AI?** In offices in Nairobi, apartments in Manila, shared housing in Bangalore, there are people reading texts we do not want to read, making judgments we do not want to make, accepting salaries we would not accept. Their names will not appear in papers, their faces will not appear at launches. But without them, none of this would happen.

**Is the price fair?** $1.32/hour annotating harmful content to protect hundreds of millions of users' mental health—is this exchange fair? 700 customer service representatives replaced by a single AI assistant, and the company's cost savings become shareholder profits—is this redistribution fair? Annotators have no right to refuse harmful content because refusal means unemployment—is this situation fair? A freelance translator's rates are pushed by AI to unsustainable levels—is this market outcome fair? An artist who has done illustration for ten years is told "AI has learned your style"—is this "side effect" of technological progress fair?

**Who should be responsible?** The companies developing models, the companies using APIs, the governments setting policy—all three bear responsibility, and all three are evading it. Companies say "we hired through legitimate outsourcing channels," companies say "we are simply using the best tools," governments say "we need further study." Each layer evades, each layer can say "this is not entirely my responsibility"—and so responsibility evaporates in the chain.

And annotators and displaced workers—they have no seats. Not on OpenAI's board of directors, not at AI ethics seminars, not at labor law revision hearings. They are the most silent group in the AI age—not because they have no voice, but because no one is listening.

The severity of this problem is compounded by its global nature. AI companies are in the United States and Europe, annotators are in Africa and Southeast Asia, displaced workers are everywhere. No single country's government can solve this problem alone—it requires transnational coordination. But transnational coordination has already proven extremely difficult in the climate change domain, and the priority of the AI labor justice issue on the international agenda is far below that of climate change.

We can ask an even more fundamental question: **is the AI industry creating a new form of labor—one that belongs neither to traditional employment nor to freelance, but a "third type of labor" that is completely controlled by platforms, completely priced by algorithms, and completely suppressed by globalized supply?** If so, existing labor law frameworks—whether American or European—cannot protect these workers. What we need is not patchwork amendments to existing laws, but the creation of an entirely new protective framework for an unprecedented labor relationship.

A truly labor-justice-inclusive AI ethics framework should achieve at least three things: **First**, incorporate supply chain labor conditions into the ethical audit scope of AI systems—auditing not only whether model outputs are fair, but also whether the training process is fair. This means AI companies, when publishing models, should disclose the labor conditions of their training data annotation—similar to how listed companies disclose their supply chains. **Second**, establish transition mechanisms for workers displaced by AI—not the empty rhetoric of "learn AI," but actionable retraining, severance, and social security plans. Every time a company uses AI to replace a position, it should provide that position's worker with transition compensation equivalent to N months' salary (the specific amount to be determined by legislation). **Third**, grant annotators the right to informed consent and refusal—any annotator, before signing a contract, should clearly know what content they will face, and have the right to refuse tasks beyond their capacity without losing income.

These three things are not technical problems. They are political problems, institutional problems, value-choice problems. Every historical advance in labor justice—the eight-hour workday, the minimum wage, the prohibition of child labor—was not the result of natural market evolution, but the result of worker struggle and social choice. Labor justice in the AI age will not arrive automatically—it needs to be demanded, fought for, and legislated.

And the core of the value choice is: **do we acknowledge that those who pay the price for AI deserve to be well treated by the society AI serves?**

What the historian needs to record is not how smart AI is—but whose labor AI's intelligence is built upon, whether that labor is treated fairly, and when these people are no longer needed, whether we still remember they existed.

Because what we choose not to see will eventually be seen through another means—either through institutional improvement or through the eruption of conflict. Silence is not the solution—silence is part of the problem.

And what this diviner can do is to record these projections on the record—for those who have no seats, leaving evidence that they once existed.
