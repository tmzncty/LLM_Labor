# Labelers' Awakening

> In January 2023, *TIME* magazine's investigative report gave the world its first look at the plight of Kenyan annotators. From that point on, these workers hidden at the very bottom of the AI industry chain began raising their voices. They shared working conditions on social media, told their stories in international media, and became subjects of academic research. But awakening does not equal liberation—the structural dilemmas annotators face will not disappear simply because they have been seen.

## I. The Moment of Being Seen

On January 18, 2023, *TIME* magazine published Billy Perrigo's investigative report, revealing details of OpenAI's employment of annotators in Kenya through Sama.[^1]

The report's core data—hourly wages of $1.32-$2, daily exposure to violent and pornographic content, high PTSD rates—was widely picked up by global media. Overnight, "annotators" went from an unknown profession to a focal point of AI ethics discussions.

**The report's impact:**

- **Media attention**: BBC, CNN, *The Guardian*, and other mainstream outlets followed up
- **Academic research**: Multiple academic papers on annotators' working conditions were published
- **Policy discussions**: The US and EU began discussing regulation of the AI annotation industry
- **Public awareness**: Ordinary users for the first time realized what AI's "safety" was bought with

But the report also brought unexpected consequences. Sama had terminated its contract with OpenAI before the report was published, partly due to internal concerns about employee mental health.[^1] But terminating the contract didn't improve annotators' conditions—they simply moved from one outsourcing company to another.

## II. Annotators' Own Voices

After the *TIME* report, more and more annotators began speaking out through various channels.

**Kenyan annotators' media voice:**

Annotator A (pseudonym) told *The Guardian* in an interview:[^2]

> "Before, we couldn't tell anyone what we were doing. We had signed NDAs. But now the whole world knows. It makes us feel—I don't know how to say—seen?"

Annotator B (pseudonym) wrote on social media:

> "I worked for OpenAI for two years. I've read written descriptions of children being raped. I've read detailed suicide methods. I received no counseling. Now they say they 'care about contractors' well-being.' It makes me want to laugh."

**Filipino Remotasks annotators' sharing:**

In 2024, Remotasks annotators in the Philippines began sharing working conditions on Reddit and Facebook groups.[^3]

One annotator wrote:

> "I've worked on Remotasks for three years. In the beginning, I could earn $2-3 per hour. Now the pay for the same tasks has been halved. If you won't do it, there are plenty of people who will."

Another annotator wrote:

> "I'm an engineer. In the Philippines, an engineer's salary isn't enough to support a family. I started doing annotation work on Remotasks. It pays more than any local job I could find. But I also know that this $100-200/month is a fraction of what a Silicon Valley company pays an engineer."

## III. Structural Dilemmas

Annotators' awakening faces a fundamental obstacle: **structural dilemmas do not disappear simply because they are seen.**

**Dilemma One: Remote and dispersed**

Annotators are not in the same factory, do not know each other, and do not share physical space. Their "workplace" is their own bedroom; their "colleagues" are anonymous usernames on a platform.[^4]

This dispersion makes traditional union organizing nearly impossible. An annotator in Nairobi and an annotator in Manila, even if doing identical work, have no way to establish contact.

**Dilemma Two: Independent contractor status**

Annotators are "independent contractors," not "employees." This means:[^5]

- No minimum wage protection
- No overtime pay
- No health insurance
- No pension
- No paid leave
- No workplace injury compensation
- No union rights
- No anti-discrimination protections

More importantly, "independent contractor" status excludes annotators from the scope of labor protections. Traditional unions' collective bargaining rights typically apply only to employment relationships.

**Dilemma Three: Globalized labor supply**

If Kenyan annotators took collective action—such as striking or demanding higher wages—AI companies could easily redirect tasks to the Philippines or India.[^6]

Globalized labor supply strips local collective action of its leverage. Workers in one place awakened? No matter—switch to a place that hasn't awakened yet.

**Dilemma Four: Information asymmetry**

Annotators don't know where their labor is being used, don't know how much value their labor creates, and don't know how their judgments affect the model's behavior.[^7]

This information asymmetry prevents annotators from evaluating their own labor's worth. They only know that for every task completed, they earn a few cents.

## IV. The Possibility of Digital Unions

With traditional unions unviable, some new organizational forms are being attempted.

**Turkopticon:**

Turkopticon is a browser extension designed for Amazon Mechanical Turk workers, allowing them to rate and review task requesters.[^8]

The tool's logic: through information sharing, workers can avoid bad requesters, thereby improving working conditions to some degree. But it has a fundamental limitation—it cannot change the platform's pricing mechanisms or labor relations.

**DAIR Institute:**

The DAIR Institute (Distributed AI Research Institute), founded by Timnit Gebru, is dedicated to researching AI's impact on society, including annotators' working conditions.[^9]

DAIR's work includes:
- Publishing research reports on labor conditions in the annotation industry
- Providing legal and policy advocacy for annotators
- Pushing AI companies to improve annotators' working conditions

**Academic research support:**

An increasing number of scholars are focusing on annotators' working conditions.[^10]

- Mary Gray and Siddharth Suri's *Ghost Work* (2019) systematically described the plight of crowdwork labor
- Julian Posada's research focuses on the survival conditions of Venezuelan annotators
- Multiple research papers on annotators' mental health have been published

These studies provide academic backing for annotator rights advocacy.

## V. The Limits of Awakening

Awakening is necessary, but not sufficient.

**The decay of media attention:**

After the *TIME* report, global media attention to annotator issues lasted for several months. But as the news cycle faded, public attention gradually waned.[^11]

Annotators' conditions did not fundamentally change because of one report. Sama terminated its contract with OpenAI, but OpenAI's annotation needs simply shifted to other companies and other countries.

**The persistence of structural problems:**

The structural dilemmas annotators face—remote, dispersed, independent contractors, global supply—have not changed at all. These dilemmas are not caused by the malice of individual companies, but by the entire industry's business model.

**The difficulty of collective action:**

Even when annotators are aware of their situation, collective action remains extremely difficult. They have no unions, no collective bargaining rights, no legal protections. Even if they organize, the platform can easily replace them with other workers.

## Assessment

Do annotators have any possibility of collective action?

This is a dispiriting question. The answer is: under the current structure, it is nearly impossible.

Traditional collective action—unions, strikes, collective bargaining—depends on several prerequisites:
1. Workers operate in the same physical space
2. Workers have stable employment relationships
3. Workers have legally protected collective bargaining rights
4. Workers are costly to replace

Annotators' circumstances break every one of these prerequisites. They work remotely, are scattered across the globe, are "independent contractors" rather than "employees," and can be easily replaced.

Digital unions (such as Turkopticon) offer a new organizational form, but they face a fundamental dilemma: **information sharing cannot change power structures.** Workers can learn which requester is bad, but they cannot change the platform's pricing mechanism.

Academic research (such as DAIR Institute's work) provides intellectual support, but knowledge cannot be directly converted into power. Research can reveal problems, but it cannot solve them.

What is needed for real change?

What is needed is not more awakening—awakening has already happened. What is needed is **institutional change:**
1. Bringing annotators within the scope of labor protections—whether they are "independent contractors" or "employees"
2. Requiring algorithmic transparency—annotators have the right to know how their labor is being used
3. Establishing cross-border labor protection mechanisms—annotators are in Kenya, AI companies in the US; international cooperation is required

But the obstacles to these institutional changes are enormous. AI companies have powerful lobbying forces; politicians are dazzled by the narrative of "technological progress"; displaced workers have no political influence.

Annotators' awakening is the first step. But from awakening to liberation, there is still a long road to travel. And the end of that road is, for now, nowhere in sight.
