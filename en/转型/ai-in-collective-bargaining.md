# Putting AI Into the Contract: Consent, Review, Compensation, and Job Protection Through Collective Bargaining

> **As of 2026-09-04. Evidence status: 2025–2026 agreements are documented in primary union materials from SAG-AFTRA, WGA East, Unifor, NABET-CWA, and UFCW.**

One concrete way to govern AI at work is not to settle an abstract argument about whether technology is good or bad, but to write conditions of use into an enforceable labor agreement.

By 2026 this is no longer only a digital-replica issue for performers. Collective agreements in newsrooms, telecommunications clerical work, broadcast work, and retail bookselling are using different AI provisions: **consent and compensation, human review, advance notice, joint committees, additional severance when AI directly causes layoffs, and explicit job or hours protections.**

This does not show that AI has caused the same degree of displacement in these workplaces, or that every clause will work as intended. It does show that AI has entered negotiated labor-relations texts across several sectors.

## SAG-AFTRA: consent and compensation around digital replicas

SAG-AFTRA members approved the 2025 Interactive Media Agreement by 95.04% to 4.96%, concluding the related video-game strike.[^1]

Union materials described AI provisions including:

- informed-consent requirements across multiple uses;
- disclosure and compensation rules for digital replicas;
- an ability to suspend consent for generation of new material with a digital replica during a strike;
- collectively bargained minimum compensation for certain replica and real-time-generation uses.[^2]

The agreement did not ban all AI. It turned the dispute into specific questions: who can use a performer's voice, likeness, or performance, for what purpose, for how long, with what consent, and at what price?

SAG-AFTRA members then approved the 2026 TV/Theatrical contracts by 91.42% in June 2026. The union said the deal strengthened earlier digital-replica protections and added guardrails against synthetic material replacing human performance.[^3]

AI terms were therefore not merely a one-off strike settlement. They were revised again in the next major contract cycle.

## HuffPost: putting mandatory human review into news production

In February 2026, the 69-member WGA East bargaining unit at HuffPost unanimously ratified its fourth three-year collective agreement. The union's primary account states that the contract:

- guarantees human review of all published content, including AI-generated story summaries;
- requires advance notice before new AI tools are implemented;
- bars AI impersonation of employees without consent;
- provides an additional three weeks of severance if AI is a **direct cause** of layoffs;
- maintains a standards-desk AI policy and an AI working group including unit members.[^4]

The phrase “direct cause” matters. The contract does not automatically classify every layoff as AI-caused; it builds a causal threshold into the compensation trigger. This is a useful reminder that collective bargaining, like research, must distinguish technology adoption, organizational restructuring, and layoff causality.

The HuffPost provisions also move beyond training-data ownership. They address whether AI output can bypass human editors, whether employees receive notice of new tools, and what compensation follows if substitution becomes a direct cause of job loss.

## Bell Clerical: governing introduction through a joint committee

In January 2026, Unifor announced a new four-year Bell Clerical agreement covering about 3,000 members. It established a **Joint Committee on Artificial Intelligence** to review the introduction of AI in the workplace and strengthened consultation requirements for technological change.[^5]

This provision does not ban AI, and it does not demonstrate that Bell has already replaced a particular number of jobs with AI. It addresses an earlier layer of the adoption process: **who gets notice, consultation, and an institutional role when new systems enter work.**

Joint-committee language is especially useful for studying the middle layer between capability and employment outcomes. It shows that employer adoption can itself become a continuing subject of labor relations rather than a purely managerial technical decision.

## NABET-CWA / ABC-TV: generative-AI guardrails in a broadcast master agreement

In June 2026, NABET-CWA members ratified a new Master Agreement with ABC-TV covering broadcast technicians, newswriters, producers, and desk assistants in New York, Chicago, Washington, Los Angeles, and San Francisco. The contract takes effect on October 1, 2026 and runs through March 31, 2031.[^6]

CWA's official summary confirms that the agreement includes guardrails concerning how generative AI will affect members. The union says the issue is particularly important for graphic artists and newswriters but that the protections apply across the covered membership.[^6]

The public summary does not disclose the full AI language. It is therefore appropriate to record that generative-AI protections entered the master agreement, but **not** to invent notice periods, prohibited uses, compensation rates, or enforcement procedures that the source does not publish.

## Solid State Books: an explicit anti-displacement clause in a small retail unit

In September 2026, UFCW International published details of a three-year agreement covering seven Local 400 members at the H Street location of Solid State Books in Washington, D.C. UFCW says the language recognizes that AI may enhance bargaining-unit work but may not replace or displace human labor, and bars termination, layoff, demotion, or reductions in regularly scheduled hours as a result of introducing, implementing, or expanding AI technologies.[^7]

The unit is tiny, so this cannot be treated as evidence that U.S. retail workers generally have AI job guarantees. It matters because the bargaining target moves from performer replicas and newsroom review to direct employment outcomes: **jobs, rank, and scheduled hours.**

UFCW also says the AI wording was supplied by its International National Bargaining Department. At minimum, this shows that such language is not conceptually confined to Hollywood-scale bargaining.

## An emerging contractual toolkit

Taken together, these agreements reveal at least six different bargaining targets:

1. **Consent and permitted uses** — whether AI may use a worker's voice, likeness, performance, or identity.
2. **Compensation** — how replicas, generated uses, or layoffs directly caused by AI are priced.
3. **Human review** — whether AI output may enter a finished product without a person reviewing it.
4. **Notice and consultation** — whether workers receive advance notice, bargaining, or joint-committee review before adoption.
5. **Job protection** — whether AI may trigger termination, layoff, demotion, or reduced hours.
6. **Continuing governance** — AI policies, working groups, and joint committees that can adapt after the contract is signed.

This is more precise than asking whether unions are “for” or “against” AI. The negotiated object is often not a master switch, but a set of **rights over adoption, workflow, price, review, and responsibility.**

## Why individual consent is still not the whole issue

A worker clicking “I agree” does not necessarily have bargaining power. Collective agreements differ from platform terms because conditions are negotiated across a workforce and can be tied to minimum compensation, permitted uses, notice, review, job protection, and grievance procedures.

AI labor protection is therefore not only a privacy question. It is also a question of labor price, workflow control, and allocation of employment risk.

## What remains unknown

1. Contract language does not show that these workplaces have already adopted AI at scale, much less that AI has caused a measurable number of job losses.
2. Written protections do not guarantee effective enforcement; grievances, arbitration, and implementation outcomes require longitudinal observation.
3. The Solid State Books unit has only seven covered members and cannot stand in for retail as a sector. NABET-CWA's public account does not publish the full AI clauses.
4. These examples come from organized workplaces with collective-bargaining infrastructure. Many annotators, platform contractors, freelancers, and white-collar workers lack comparable bargaining power.
5. Proving that AI was the “direct cause” of a layoff may itself become a future contract-interpretation and causality dispute.
6. AI changes faster than many contract cycles, so definitions, scopes, and exceptions will continue to need revision.

## Analysis

The important 2025–2026 change is not that unions have settled whether AI is good or bad. It is that labor relations are breaking AI into questions that can be written into clauses.

Performers bargain over replicas, consent, and price. News workers bargain over human review and AI-caused layoff severance. Telecommunications clerical workers bargain over a joint committee. Broadcast workers negotiate generative-AI guardrails. Booksellers bargain over termination, demotion, and reductions in hours.

AI is moving from corporate strategy and ethics statements into pay, workflow, grievance processes, and collective agreements—the parts of labor institutions that leave durable historical traces.
