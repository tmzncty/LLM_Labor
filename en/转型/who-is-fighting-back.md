# Who Is Fighting Back: From Individual Refusal to Collective Bargaining

> **As of 2026-09-08. Status: REVISED.** AI labor conflict matures when workers move from generic opposition to enforceable rules on consent, training, compensation, review, and collective action demands.

## Collective bargaining
SAG-AFTRA's 2025–2026 agreements expanded protections around digital replicas, synthetics, informed consent, compensation and AI training. The WGA's 2026 MBA requires written notice when covered writing is licensed to train a commercial generative-AI system and allows the Guild to seek discussion, including remuneration.[^1][^2][^3]

## Organizing data workers
Africa Tech Workers Rising in Kenya organizes content moderators and data-enrichment workers around pay, workload, health and safety, whistleblower protections and worker voice, while building a wider African network.[^4]

## Internal resistance can constrain automation
Reuters reported that Meta's 2026 Project OT envisioned deep AI-driven team cuts, but employee backlash plus disappointing productivity, reliability and security results forced the company to scale back further reductions.[^5]

## University of Sydney: whether AI rules belong in an enforceable agreement becomes a strike issue

On September 2, 2026, members of Australia's National Tertiary Education Union (NTEU) at the University of Sydney held a 24-hour protected strike. AI protections were one bargaining deadlock, but **not the sole cause**: the action also covered workload fairness, job security, disability rights, change-management protections, professional-staff conditions, and pay.[^6]

The event matters for AI labor history because AI governance had moved into bargaining over enforceable Enterprise Agreement rights. The branch's earlier AI proposal called for AI to serve the public interest and enhance rather than replace work; explainable and transparent systems with contestable decisions; human judgment in employment decisions; staff control over personal data and an ability to opt out of training-data collection; moral rights in intellectual property; and consultation before AI systems are introduced.[^7]

The University's counterposition is equally important. In its September 2 response, the University said it supported a human-centred approach to AI and already had a Responsible AI Framework, principles, and guardrails, but argued that many objectives were more appropriately implemented through adaptable policies rather than the Enterprise Agreement.[^8]

The conflict should therefore not be reduced to “union against AI, management for AI.” One substantive dispute is **where governance becomes enforceable**: in management policies that can evolve, or in collectively bargained rights backed by an agreement and dispute process. As of September 8, the union has not won the proposed AI clauses, and the strike cannot be attributed wholly or primarily to AI on the available evidence.

The emerging conflict is therefore less “AI or no AI” than: who gets notice, consent, human review, bargaining rights and compensation—and which protections become enforceable rather than remaining management policy.

## What we cannot claim
These cases do not show that most AI workers are unionized or protected. Cross-border data work remains highly fragmented. Nor does the Sydney strike show that AI alone caused the industrial action or that the proposed AI protections have already entered a final agreement.
