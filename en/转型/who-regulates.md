# Who Regulates

> AI is transforming the nature of labor, but laws and institutions haven't kept up. Does an annotator's PTSD count as a workplace injury? Do gig economy "independent contractors" have minimum wage protections? When AI replaces human jobs, who picks up the pieces? Governments around the world are groping for answers—some are legislating, some are watching, some are embracing AI companies. Regulation is far slower than technology.

## I. The Dilemma of Regulation

AI labor regulation faces a fundamental dilemma: **technology moves too fast; law responds too slowly.**

AI's iteration cycle is measured in months. A model can go from release to widespread adoption in just weeks. But the legislative cycle spans years—from drafting to debate, revision, voting, and implementation typically requires 2-5 years.

This means: by the time the law is enacted, it may already be obsolete.

**Another dilemma is globalization.** Annotators are in Kenya, AI companies in the US, annotation platforms in Singapore, data stored in Ireland. Which country's law applies?[^1]

## II. The United States: Watching and Fragmented

The US has no federal-level AI labor legislation. Regulation is fragmented—each state acts on its own.

**California:**
- AB5 (effective 2020): Limits the use of "independent contractors," requiring businesses to treat qualifying workers as "employees"[^2]
- But Uber, Lyft, and other companies obtained exemptions through Prop 22 (2020 ballot initiative)[^3]
- Whether annotation platforms (such as Remotasks) are subject to AB5 remains undetermined

**New York City:**
- In July 2023, NYC Local Law 144 took effect: requiring businesses using AI recruitment tools to undergo "bias audits"[^4]
- This is the first US legislation targeting AI in hiring
- But it does not address AI's impact on existing employment

**Federal level:**
- The Biden administration issued an AI executive order in October 2023, calling for "protecting workers from AI-driven harms"[^5]
- But executive orders are not legally binding
- At the congressional level, multiple AI-related bills have failed to pass

**Core problem**: The US labor law framework—centered on the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA)—was enacted in 1938.[^6] This law assumes workers are "employees" working at fixed locations, fixed hours, for fixed employers. It does not apply to crowdworkers, gig workers, or remote annotators.

## III. The European Union: Leading in Legislation

The EU is at the forefront of global AI regulation.

**The AI Act (passed 2024):**

The EU AI Act is the world's first comprehensive AI regulatory framework.[^7] While primarily focused on AI system safety and risk levels, it also touches on the labor domain:

- "High-risk" AI systems (including those used in recruitment and human resources management) must undergo compliance assessments
- AI systems must be transparent—workers have the right to know if they are being evaluated by AI
- AI systems posing "unacceptable risk" (such as real-time biometric surveillance) are banned in the workplace

**Platform Work Directive (passed 2024):**

This is EU legislation specifically targeting the platform economy (including crowdwork platforms):[^8]

- Presumes platform workers to be "employees" (rather than independent contractors), unless the platform can prove otherwise
- Requires algorithmic transparency—platforms must explain to workers how algorithms assign tasks, evaluate performance, and determine compensation
- Prohibits algorithmic firing decisions—dismissals must be made by humans
- Requires platforms to provide occupational safety and health protections

**Impact**: If Scale AI's Remotasks operated in the EU, it might be required to treat annotators as "employees," providing minimum wages, social insurance, and labor protections.[^9]

## IV. China: Development First

China's AI regulation focuses on content security and data security, with less attention to AI labor issues.

**"Interim Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services" (effective August 2023):**[^10]
- Primarily concerned with the safety of AI-generated content
- Requires AI service providers to take responsibility for generated content
- Does not address AI's impact on the labor market

**Labor law level:**
- China's "Labor Law" and "Labor Contract Law" similarly assume workers are "employees"
- Labor relationship classification for gig economy workers (food delivery riders, ride-hailing drivers) remains contentious[^11]
- There are no clear legal guidelines for annotators' labor relationship classification

**Industry self-regulation:**
- The China Artificial Intelligence Industry Development Alliance issued an "AI Industry Self-Regulation Convention"[^12]
- Primarily focused on data security and privacy protection
- Does not address AI labor issues

## V. Kenya: The Silence of the Exploited

Kenya is a major hub of the global AI annotation industry, but its regulatory capacity is nearly nonexistent.

**Background:**
- Kenya's minimum wage: approximately $1.15/hour (varies by industry and region)[^13]
- Annotator hourly wage: $1.32-$2—above minimum wage, but far below US standards
- High unemployment rate; young people lack employment options

**Regulatory vacuum:**
- No dedicated AI labor law
- Labor law provides limited protection for "independent contractors"
- Annotators have no unions and no collective bargaining rights

**Subsequent developments:**
- In 2023, the Kenyan government began investigating Sama's labor practices[^14]
- The investigation found that Sama failed to provide mental health support for employees, paid below-standard hourly wages, and exceeded legal working hour limits
- But the investigation results have not been published, and Sama has not been penalized

## VI. International Organizations' Calls

**International Labour Organization (ILO):**

In 2024, the ILO published a report on "Decent Work in the Platform Economy":[^15]
- Called on countries to bring platform workers under labor protections
- Called for algorithmic transparency and accountability
- Called for cross-border labor protection mechanisms

**The United Nations:**

UN Secretary-General António Guterres stated in 2024:[^16]
> "AI has the potential to exacerbate global inequality. We must ensure that AI development serves all people, not just a handful of companies and countries."

## Assessment

The core problem of global AI labor regulation is: **who will protect the invisible workers?**

The answer is dispiriting: almost no one.

The United States' labor law framework was enacted in 1938—it doesn't apply to 21st-century crowdwork. The EU is leading, but its legislative speed is far slower than technological development. China prioritizes AI development, with limited attention to labor protections. Kenya—the hub of the annotation industry—has virtually no regulatory capacity.

This regulatory vacuum is not accidental. It serves the AI industry's interests. If annotators are classified as "employees," they are entitled to minimum wages, social insurance, and mental health support—all of which would dramatically increase AI's training costs. If algorithms must be transparent, annotation platforms cannot squeeze costs by adjusting task pay rates. If countries regulate strictly, AI companies will move annotation operations to countries with laxer regulation.

The AI industry's global layout is a form of **regulatory arbitrage**: wherever annotation is cheapest and regulation is loosest, that's where it happens. Kenya, the Philippines, Venezuela—these countries share common traits: cheap labor, weak regulation, feeble unions.

The EU's Platform Work Directive is an important step forward. It presumes platform workers to be "employees," requires algorithmic transparency, and prohibits algorithmic firing. If rigorously enforced, it could reshape the global annotation landscape.

But the question is: can EU legislation affect annotators in Kenya? The answer is very likely no. Scale AI can place annotation operations outside the EU, beyond the reach of EU law.

The real challenge for AI labor regulation is **cross-border enforcement.** Annotators are in Kenya, AI companies in the US, annotation platforms in Singapore. Which country's law applies? Who enforces it?

This is a global problem requiring a global solution. But currently, no international institution has the capability or authority to implement such a solution.

The International Labour Organization (ILO) calls for "decent work" and "algorithmic transparency," but the ILO's recommendations carry no legal force. The United Nations calls to "ensure AI serves all," but the United Nations cannot stop an American company from hiring annotators at $1.32/hour in Kenya.

The absence of regulation is not because technology is too complex. It is because the interests are too powerful. The AI industry profits from regulatory gaps—lower costs, greater flexibility, less responsibility. And annotators—the people bearing the greatest risk—lose everything in those gaps. (See *On Labor Justice*.) They lose everything.
