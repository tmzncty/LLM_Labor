# Who Regulates: AI Labor Rules Move from Principles Toward Enforcement

> **As of 2026-09-04.** Workplace-AI and algorithmic-management regulation is becoming more concrete: automated decisions, human review, excessive monitoring, transparency, training-data responsibility, and high-risk employment systems. Actual enforcement, however, does not come only from the AI Act. Existing data-protection law can already constrain automated management that affects workers' access to income and work.

## EU workplace rules: several clocks are running at once

The European Commission's July 2026 second-stage consultation on a Quality Jobs Act explicitly covers algorithmic management and AI at work, human-centered automated decisions, excessive monitoring, subcontracting-chain rights, and skills transitions.[^1]

This remains a legislative and social-partner consultation process, not a fully applicable EU labor statute.

From 2 August 2026, a set of AI Act transparency rules entered application and enforcement.[^2] But “the AI Act is being enforced” does **not** mean every workplace AI system is already under the full high-risk regime.

### Already applicable: workplace emotion-recognition prohibition

Article 5 prohibits AI systems used to infer a person's emotions in workplace settings from biometric data, subject to narrow medical or safety exceptions.[^5] Commission guidance interprets workplace broadly and includes recruitment, probation, call centers, and other physical or virtual work settings.[^6]

### Already applicable: AI literacy

Article 4 has applied since February 2025. Commission guidance says providers and deployers should take measures supporting AI literacy for staff and other people operating AI on their behalf, with training appropriate to knowledge, context, and use.[^7]

### Later: Annex III employment high-risk obligations

Annex III classifies systems used for recruitment and selection, decisions affecting employment relationships, promotion and termination, task allocation based on individual behavior, and performance monitoring or evaluation as high risk.[^8]

Following the 2026 Digital Omnibus implementation changes, the Annex III high-risk requirements apply from **2 December 2027**. High-risk systems embedded in regulated products follow in August 2028.[^3]

The accurate status as of 2026-09-04 is therefore mixed: some prohibitions, governance, transparency, and literacy rules already apply, while the full Annex III employment regime is still transitional.

## Algorithmic management is already here

OECD employer evidence across France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Spain, and the United States shows algorithmic management is widespread and raises concerns about accountability, explainability, and worker health; worker consultation can mitigate risks.[^4]

ILO defines algorithmic management as systems using tracked data and other information to organize, assign, monitor, supervise, and evaluate work, and notes that it now reaches beyond platforms into customer service, transport and logistics, banking, and health care.[^9]

These sources document adoption. They do not by themselves establish layoffs, wage reductions, or illegality.

## The Dutch Uber case: automated management reaches actual enforcement

On 21 August 2026, the Dutch Data Protection Authority, Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (AP), announced a **€824,990,000** fine against Uber. The AP found that software automatically made temporary or permanent driver-account deactivation decisions in situations involving suspected fraud or customer ratings, without meaningful human assessment before the consequential decision. While accounts were deactivated, drivers could not earn income through Uber. The regulator found that the practice violated GDPR restrictions on solely automated decisions with significant effects.[^10]

For labor research, the important point is that human review has moved from an ethics principle into an enforcement issue with direct consequences: **may an automated system cut off a worker's access to a platform and income without genuine human intervention?**

Three limits are essential:

1. **This is GDPR enforcement, not enforcement of the AI Act's employment high-risk regime.**
2. **The core issue is automated decision-making / algorithmic management; the system need not be generative AI.** The case is therefore not evidence that “generative AI illegally fired drivers.”
3. **A regulator's decision is not a final judicial outcome.** Uber has appealed.

Reuters reported Uber's counter-position: the company disputes parts of the regulator's description of its deactivation process, says account actions included human review and appeal mechanisms, and contests the characterization of permanent deactivations as fully automated.[^11]

The strongest current evidence statement is therefore two-sided: **the AP's fine and findings are documented regulator facts; Uber's appeal and factual objections are also documented; the ultimate court outcome remains unresolved.**

This is also useful counterevidence against regulatory overstatement. Enforcement is becoming real, but a very large fine does not convert every disputed technical fact or legal interpretation into a final judgment.

## Regulation is not only legislation

Collective bargaining, union contracts, sector rules, regulators, and courts all contribute to workplace-AI governance. WGA, SAG-AFTRA, WGA East, Unifor, NABET-CWA, and UFCW agreements now use different combinations of notice, consent, human review, joint committees, compensation, and job protection.

These mechanisms are complementary rather than interchangeable:

- legislation can set minimum rights around automated decisions;
- collective agreements can add workflow-specific notice, review, compensation, and employment protections;
- regulators and courts determine how rules operate in actual disputes.

## What we know

1. Some AI Act workplace-related prohibitions and governance duties already apply, while the Annex III employment high-risk regime still has a later implementation date.
2. Algorithmic management is an observed workplace practice, not merely a capability demonstration.
3. The 2026 Dutch AP decision against Uber provides an A-tier regulator case in which automated account deactivation, human intervention, and access to income are directly at issue under GDPR.
4. Collective agreements are adding workplace-specific protections beyond general statutory rules.

## What remains unknown

1. The final outcome of Uber's appeal and whether a court will uphold all of the AP's factual and legal findings.
2. Whether legal text and large penalties will broadly change algorithmic-management practices across platforms and conventional employers.
3. How employers will redesign recruitment, evaluation, termination, and task-allocation systems when Annex III employment requirements actually apply in 2027.
4. Whether cross-border outsourcing, independent contractors, small platforms, and multi-tier supply chains will receive equivalent practical protection.
5. How much substantive authority a human reviewer must have before “human review” is more than a rubber stamp.

The next useful historical evidence will not be another announcement that an AI law exists. It will be evidence about **specific systems, specific employment decisions, specific human-review procedures, and what regulators, arbitrators, and courts actually do with them.**
