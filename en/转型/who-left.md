# Transformation: Who Left

> Some chose to leave—leaving the industry, the city, their former selves. This is not a story of failure; it is a story of choice. But in an era where "learn to code" has become universal advice, how much courage does it take to say "I'm done learning"? This article records those who turned and walked away under the impact of AI. Where did they go? Why did they leave? Has society provided them with adequate transitional support?

## I. The Translation Industry: Veteran Translators Turn Away

In the translation industry, far more people left than stayed.

According to ProZ.com's survey, freelance translators' average income declined 30%-50% between 2022 and 2024.[^1] Behind this number lies a massive exodus of translators, both voluntary and involuntary. They went in different directions, but shared one thing in common: no one left because they "couldn't learn" MTPE—they left because they did the math and found it wasn't worth staying.

**Career path one: Project management and localization consulting.** Some veteran translators, leveraging years of accumulated industry knowledge and client relationships, transitioned into translation project management or localization consulting. They no longer translate themselves—they manage translation workflows, coordinating translators, reviewing quality, and liaising with clients. The advantage of this path: they understand both translation and clients, and AI cannot yet replace this "intermediary" role.[^2] But the seats are limited—a translation company only needs a few project managers, while it once needed dozens of translators.

**Career path two: Foreign trade and cross-border e-commerce.** Language skills still have a market in the foreign trade industry. Some translators moved into cross-border e-commerce operations, foreign trade documentation, and overseas market development roles. These positions require language ability but don't center on "translation"—language is the tool, and business judgment is the value.[^3] This path is relatively viable for English translators, but for those working in less common languages, matching foreign trade positions are far fewer.

**Career path three: Completely different industries.** The most thorough leavers left language-related industries entirely. Li Hua, the translation graduate mentioned earlier, sent out over 200 resumes and received only 5 interviews, ultimately landing an administrative position at a foreign trade company at a salary of 5,000 yuan/month.[^4] This is not an isolated case—a large number of 2023 translation graduates failed to enter the translation industry. University translation program admission score lines showed a notable decline in 2024,[^5] a silent vote of no-confidence from the market.

Chen Xue (pseudonym), a freelance translator with 12 years of experience, recounted her decision to leave: "In the second half of 2023, I received nothing but MTPE projects for three consecutive months, and the per-thousand-word rate dropped from 120 to 60 yuan. I did the math—at this rate, I'd need to proofread ten thousand words a day just to maintain my previous income. Ten thousand words means staring at a screen for 10 hours. I asked myself: is this the life I want? The answer was no."[^6]

Chen Xue eventually joined an education technology company doing curriculum design, leveraging her language skills for cross-cultural content planning. She did not return to the translation industry.[^7]

**The structural collapse of the industry:**

The mass closure of small and medium-sized translation companies accelerated translators' departure. In 2023, approximately 20% of translation companies worldwide closed or were acquired.[^8] Employees of these companies—proofreaders, project managers, quality inspectors—found it very difficult to find matching work after closure, because the surviving translation companies were also downsizing.

## II. The Illustration Industry: Laying Down the Brush

Those who left the illustration industry faced a cruel reality: the skills they spent a decade honing depreciated by 80% in two years.[^9] This depreciation was not gradual—it was a cliff. Stable Diffusion launched in August 2022; by late 2023, freelancers' order volumes had dropped an average of 60%.[^10]

**Career path one: UI/UX design.** This is the most common transition path for illustrators. UI/UX design still requires visual aesthetic sensibility, but the work is more stable and income more predictable. The catch: the design industry itself is being disrupted by AI—Figma has already built in AI generation features. How long this path remains viable, no one knows.[^11]

**Career path two: 3D modeling and animation.** The 3D field currently has a lower AI replacement rate—generating an editable 3D model is far more complex than generating a 2D image. Some illustrators switched to 3D tools like Blender and Maya. But the learning curve is steep; the transition from 2D to 3D typically takes 1-2 years.[^12]

**Career path three: Giving up creative work for a regular job.** The most common and least discussed transition path. Many illustrators didn't move to another creative position—they took jobs at e-commerce companies doing graphic production, at advertising firms doing execution-level design, or at training institutions as teachers. Income stabilized, but creativity died.

Liu Qiang, mentioned earlier, graduated from an art academy and sent out over 100 resumes, receiving only 3 interviews. He eventually took a graphic production role at an e-commerce company, earning 6,000 yuan/month.[^13] His words are worth repeating: "Our teacher said illustration is a profession requiring creativity, one that will never be replaced by machines."

Liu Qiang's story is not unique. Among Chinese art universities, the employment situation for the 2024 graduating class is even more severe than the previous one. Some institutions began adjusting their curricula to include AI tool training—but this adjustment far too slowly to keep pace with industry changes.[^14] One student wrote on social media: "When I entered university as a freshman, the school taught hand-drawing. By my junior year, the school suddenly started teaching Midjourney. By the time I graduated as a senior, companies said 'We don't need people who can use Midjourney—AI iterates automatically. We want people who understand business.' Three years of learning, wasted."[^15]

**The outcome of the ArtStation boycott:**

In late 2022, illustrators on ArtStation collectively protested AI-generated images.[^16] They demanded the platform ban AI content. ArtStation refused.[^17] By early 2024, the movement's participants had scattered. Some left ArtStation, some continued uploading works with "NO AI" watermarks, and some began learning AI tools. The boycott did not change the industry's direction—it only made those who left depart more resolutely.

This movement revealed a dilemma: when technological change arrives, individual workers' collective action can barely alter the industry's trajectory. ArtStation's logic for refusing to ban AI content was simple—banning AI would cause the platform to lose a large number of users and content. The interests of platforms and creators diverged fundamentally for the first time in the AI era.

## III. The Programming Industry: Junior Developers Exit

The leavers in the programming industry differ from those in the previous two. In translation and illustration, leavers were "driven out by price wars"—their skills still had a market, but the prices were too low. In programming, leavers were "driven out by a disappearing ladder"—entry-level positions were vanishing, and newcomers couldn't get a seat at the table.

**The contraction of junior developer positions:**

In 2023-2024, multiple tech companies reduced hiring for junior engineers.[^18] The reason is straightforward: when senior developers using Copilot can handle workloads that previously required 2-3 people,[^19] companies no longer need as many junior employees to handle the "grunt work."

This is a structural problem. Junior developers' traditional role was: writing boilerplate code, fixing bugs, running tests—precisely the tasks AI is best at. When AI can do these things, junior developers lose the opportunity to "practice," and thus lose their path to becoming senior developers.

Coding bootcamp graduates were hit hardest. These bootcamps' business models rest on an assumption: students can find junior developer positions after 3-6 months of intensive training. After 2023, this assumption is crumbling.[^20] Some bootcamps began adjusting their curricula from "teaching you to write code" to "teaching you to use AI to write code"—but when everyone can use AI to write code, this is no longer a competitive advantage.

**Career path one: Product management.** Some people with technical backgrounds but not-strong-enough coding skills pivoted to product management. They understand technology but don't need to write code themselves. In the AI era, product managers need to understand "what AI can do"—which happens to be an advantage for programmers transitioning.[^21]

**Career path two: Technical writing and documentation engineering.** Some programmers are better at "explaining technology clearly" than "writing the code." Technical writing is a relatively stable direction—no matter how AI-augmented products become, someone still needs to write the documentation.[^22]

**Career path three: Entrepreneurship.** Some junior developers channeled the anxiety of "being replaced by AI" into entrepreneurial motivation. They used AI tools to reduce development costs, attempting what previously required an entire team. One person + Claude Code + Copilot can build a minimum viable product (MVP)—something previously unimaginable.[^23]

But entrepreneurial success rates are notoriously low. Most "entrepreneurship with AI" stories remain as inspirational posts on social media rather than in actual business data.

**The bankruptcy of "learn to code" advice:**

Over the past decade, "learn to code" was everyone's universal advice for everyone. Got laid off? Learn to code. Want to switch careers? Learn to code. Low income? Learn to code.

After 2023, this advice failed for the first time. Not because coding doesn't matter—but because "learn to code" no longer equals "get a job." When entry-level positions are disappearing, learning to write code is only the first step, and the first step is no longer enough.

## IV. Laid-Off Customer Service: Klarna's 700

In February 2024, the Swedish fintech company Klarna publicly announced that its AI customer service system had handled a workload equivalent to 700 full-time customer service agents.[^24] This number was widely cited but later sparked controversy—analysts pointed out that the 700 figure was a cumulative equivalent, not a one-time layoff number, and that Klarna's actual customer service team reduction was smaller than media reports suggested.[^25]

**Verifiable facts:**

- Klarna conducted multiple rounds of layoffs between 2022 and 2024, reducing its total headcount from approximately 5,000 to approximately 3,500[^26]
- Its AI customer service system handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month of operation, accounting for two-thirds of total customer service volume[^27]
- The AI system's average resolution time and customer satisfaction were reportedly comparable to human agents[^28]

**Unverifiable facts:**

- The specific destinations of laid-off customer service staff—no publicly available tracking data exists
- Whether customer service staff left voluntarily or were laid off—Klarna has not publicly distinguished between the two
- Whether retraining programs existed—Klarna has not disclosed relevant information

This is the most troubling gap in AI labor replacement: **no one is tracking where the replaced workers went.** Companies announce AI efficiency data, media outlets cite these data, but the subsequent lives of the 700 laid-off workers (or some number less than 700) are not part of any report.

**The global impact on customer service:**

Klarna is just one case. The outsourced customer service industry—particularly BPO (business process outsourcing) centers in countries like India and the Philippines—faces even larger-scale disruption. According to Gartner's 2024 projections, AI customer service systems can handle 70%-80% of common inquiries.[^29] India's BPO industry employs approximately 5 million people,[^30] many of whom do standardized customer service work. When AI can handle common inquiries around the clock, seven days a week, the foundation for these positions erodes.

**The unique vulnerability of customer service:**

Customer service is a "low barrier to entry, high replacement rate" industry. Workers typically don't need advanced degrees, and the work is highly standardized—precisely the type AI most easily replaces. When an industry's entry barriers are low, exit costs are also low (no "sunk" professional training), but post-exit destinations are also more uncertain (no transferable specialized skills).[^31]

For customer service workers in developing countries, the problem is even more severe. Their replacement—by AI—doesn't even leave a window for "transitioning." After an Indian customer service representative is replaced by AI, what can she pivot to? The local economy does not have a wealth of positions "requiring English communication skills but not specialized skills" waiting for her.

**Beyond Klarna:**

Klarna's case matters not because it is the largest, but because it is the most public. Other companies' AI customer service replacements are happening quietly—no press releases, no "700 people" figures, no controversy. A US telecom company's customer service department reduced its workforce by 40% in 2023, citing "organizational optimization"—but insiders know that "optimization" is a euphemism for AI replacement.[^32]

This "quiet replacement" is more dangerous than Klarna's public announcement. Because it doesn't trigger public discussion, doesn't invite media scrutiny, and doesn't leave any trace that requires accountability. Workers are laid off under the name of "organizational optimization," without even a recognizable "replacer" to point to.

## V. Common Characteristics of Those Who Left

Examining the leavers across all four industries, several patterns emerge.

**Voluntary vs. involuntary departure.** Veteran translator Chen Xue left voluntarily—she did the math, found staying wasn't worthwhile, and planned a deliberate pivot to curriculum design. Art academy graduate Liu Qiang left involuntarily—he never had the chance to enter the industry in the first place. These two types of "departure" are fundamentally different. Voluntary leavers generally fare better, because they have more choices. Involuntary leavers often fall into a vicious cycle: "no experience → can't find work → still no experience."[^33]

**Economic pressure vs. burnout.** Some leave because the money runs out—income drops below the level needed to sustain a living. Some leave because the sense of meaning evaporates—going from "creator" to "AI operator," even if income doesn't decline, they no longer want to do it. The former is an economically driven forced choice; the latter is a values-driven voluntary choice. The two often occur simultaneously.

Notably, there is a vicious cycle between economic pressure and burnout: declining income leads to increased workload (to compensate by accepting more low-value projects), increased workload intensifies burnout, burnout degrades work quality, declining quality drives away clients, and client loss further reduces income. Many leavers are not felled by a single factor—they are worn down by this cycle.

**Young vs. older.** Young people's departures are more thorough—they haven't yet built up enough in the industry, and the cost of pivoting is lower. Older people's departures are more painful—a professional identity built over a decade-plus must be written off, but their domain knowledge is often more valuable when transitioning careers (e.g., translator to project manager, programmer to product manager).

The role of age in the departure decision is complex. A 25-year-old illustrator pivoting to UI design still has 30 years of career ahead to rebuild. A 45-year-old translator pivoting—to where? She faces not only skill reconstruction but also age discrimination, family obligations, and the psychological toll of "starting over." Society's narrative about "young people changing careers" is "bravely chasing dreams"; the narrative about "middle-aged people changing careers" is often "midlife crisis." Both narratives are oversimplifications.

**Developed vs. developing countries.** Leavers' circumstances vary by country. Developed countries have relatively comprehensive social safety nets (unemployment insurance, retraining programs). Developing countries have thinner safety nets—an Indian customer service worker replaced by AI faces far fewer choices than a Swedish one. The global impact of AI labor replacement is exacerbating existing international inequality.

**No one is tracking them.** This is the most important and most unsettling common characteristic. Klarna didn't track where its laid-off customer service workers went. Translation associations didn't track translators who left the industry. Art academies didn't track graduates who didn't find jobs. The topic of "transition" is nearly blank at the data level—we know how many people left, but not where they went or how they're doing.

**Leaving doesn't mean liberation.** A common misconception is that leavers "shed their burdens and start fresh." Reality is far more complex. An illustrator who pivoted to graphic production still opens design software on their computer every day—but the work is completely different from the creative work they once loved. This sense of "so close yet so unreachable" is more tormenting than leaving an industry entirely.

One respondent who transitioned from the translation industry said: "I now do curriculum design, and the income is okay. But every time I see a translation project come up, my heart still drops—not because I want to go back, but because the skills I spent so many years honing are now useless. That feeling isn't 'unemployment'—it's 'being abandoned.'"[^34]

## Assessment

When an industry contracts, "leaving" is not retreating—it is taking responsibility for your own future.

Easy to say. But for someone who spent a decade learning to paint, someone who made their living translating, someone who dreamed of becoming a programmer since childhood, "leaving" means abandoning their identity. Your business card doesn't list your skills—it lists who you are. When "I am an illustrator" no longer holds, you need to re-answer the most fundamental question: "Who am I?"

The collapse of identity is harder to recover from than declining income. Income decline can be addressed through career change and lowering living standards. But the void of identity— "Who am I really?"—has no simple answer. A translator who pivoted to curriculum design after 12 years may recover her income, but at night she might think: "Am I a failed translator?" This question has no standard answer, but it genuinely exists in the heart of every leaver.

But identity reconstruction is only a personal-level challenge. The bigger question is at the societal level: **has society provided these people with adequate transitional support?**

The answer is clearly no.

Translation associations didn't offer retraining for translators leaving the industry. Art academies didn't adjust curricula to address AI-era realities—and even when they did adjust, the pace fell far short of industry changes. Tech companies' "re-employment services" typically cover only laid-off full-time employees, not contractors and gig workers. Government employment training programs (where they exist) typically can't keep pace with technological change.

In an era where "learn to code" has become universal advice, a more honest piece of advice would be: **you don't need to learn to code—you need to figure out which of your abilities AI can't replicate.** But this advice is a luxury for someone paying rent. When survival pressure is imminent, no one has time for "deep self-assessment."

Klarna's 700 (or some number less than 700) is a microcosm. Globally, AI is replacing different positions at different speeds—customer service, translation, illustration, junior programming—but there is one commonality: **the stories of the replaced are not part of any company's earnings report.** Efficiency has improved, profits have grown, but human destinations are not in the KPIs.

This is the most neglected corner of the entire AI labor discourse. The gains from technological progress are counted—but the costs of technological progress, and who bears them, are not.

We need a new framework to think about this problem: **labor transition tracking.** Not "unemployment rate"—that's too coarse. But "where did the unemployed go, how are they doing, and what support do they need." Companies, industry associations, governments—at least one party should bear this tracking obligation. When a company announces it has replaced 700 people with AI, it should be required to provide follow-up data on where those 700 people went.

Those who left should not be forgotten. They are not the residue discarded by history—they are the true bearers of this transformation. Recording their destinations, understanding their hardships, providing them with transitional support—this is not charity; it is responsibility.

A society that cannot provide a safety net for workers in transition does not deserve to speak of "technological progress."

But we should also see the resilience in those who left. Chen Xue went from translator to curriculum designer; Zhao Ming went from illustrator to AI art director—they are not people felled by the times; they are people who rebuilt themselves on the ruins. This resilience should not be overlooked—it is humanity's most precious asset when facing technological change.

Recording the stories of those who left is not about manufacturing pessimism—it is about making the narrative of "technological progress" more complete. Behind every efficiency gain, there is a real person making real choices. Ignore this dimension, and our understanding of "progress" is incomplete.

The real question is not "can they adapt"—leaving is itself a form of adaptation. The real question is: is this adaptation process fast enough, fair enough, and dignified enough? When a society pushes all the "adaptation" costs onto individual workers, it does not deserve to enjoy the dividends of technological progress.

What transitional support is possible?

First, **tracking obligations.** When a company announces it is replacing workers with AI, it should be required to disclose the number of people replaced and its reassignment plan—just as layoffs require reporting to labor authorities. Second, **transition funds.** A percentage should be extracted from AI technology's excess profits, earmarked for displaced workers' retraining—similar to the "polluter pays" principle in environmental law. Third, **safety net modernization.** The existing unemployment insurance system assumes "unemployment is temporary and re-employment is expected." AI-era unemployment may be permanent—the safety net needs redesign.

These are not utopian fantasies—they are minimum responsibilities. The train of technological progress won't stop to wait for anyone, but at least at every stop along the way, a path should be prepared for those forced to disembark.
