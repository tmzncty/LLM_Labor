# Transformation: Who Adapted

> Not everyone was felled by AI. Some turned AI into their own tool—translators became AI translation reviewers, illustrators used Midjourney to accelerate creation, programmers used Copilot to write more code, journalists used Claude to assist with first drafts. What they share is not extraordinary talent, but a decision that looked simple yet was actually very hard: don't resist—ride. This article records those who found their footing amid the AI shockwave, attempting to answer a critical question—what did adapters do to make adaptation possible? Is adaptation a path anyone can replicate, or is it only a privilege of the few?

## I. The Translation Industry: From Pure Human Translation to MTPE

After 2023, the biggest structural change in the translation industry wasn't the disappearance of translators—it was the disappearance of "pure human translation." Replacing it was MTPE (Machine Translation Post-Editing), a work model combining AI translation drafts with human review.[^1]

As of 2023, approximately 60% of translation projects had adopted the MTPE model.[^2] This wasn't a unilateral decision by translation companies—it was the clients who changed first. When GPT-4's translation quality reached professional translator levels in most scenarios,[^3] clients were no longer willing to pay a premium for "pure human translation."

The typical transformation path for adapters went like this:

**Step one: Accept reality.** The era of pure human translation is over. Resisting MTPE won't bring projects back—it will only cause clients to switch to a company that accepts MTPE. This step is the hardest, because it means acknowledging that the "pure translation" skills honed over five or ten years have lost most of their market value.

**Step two: Learn to review.** MTPE's skill requirements differ from pure human translation. Pure translation tests "zero-to-one" creativity; MTPE tests "eight-to-ten" diagnostic ability—you need to quickly identify systematic error patterns in machine translation (tense shifts, terminology inconsistencies, cultural mistranslations) and correct them precisely. This is not a demotion—it is a skill migration.

**Step three: Move upstream.** The most successful adapters didn't just do review—they began taking on localization project management, AI translation quality assessment, and terminology database construction. Their value was no longer "translating" but "ensuring translation quality." They went from executors to gatekeepers.[^4]

Zhao Wei (pseudonym), a translator at a major localization company, described the process: "In early 2023, I was furious—AI was stealing my livelihood. By mid-year I started learning MTPE tools, and by year's end I found my project volume had actually increased—because I could review twice as fast as pure human translation, and the company was willing to assign me more projects. Income went from a monthly average of 12,000 yuan to 8,000, but so did my working hours."[^5]

Income did decline—MTPE's per-thousand-word rate is generally 50%-70% that of pure human translation.[^6] But for translators who had mastered review skills, MTPE meant higher per-unit-time output. A person can proofread 3-5 times more words per day than they can translate.[^7] When the math is done, adapters' hourly income didn't plummet—what plummeted was the order volume for those who refused to accept MTPE.

**The cost of adaptation:**

But let us not romanticize the process. MTPE work's subjective experience is entirely different from pure human translation. Pure translation has a "creative feel"—you build a bridge between two languages, deliberating over every word choice. MTPE has an "error-correction feel"—you hunt for flaws in machine-generated text, like a quality inspector on an assembly line.

One translator who adapted to MTPE but harbored resentment said: "I used to think translation was a craft. Now I feel more like a Foxconn assembly line worker—except the product on the line is text."[^8] This identity rift is the hidden cost of adaptation.

Legal translation, literary translation, and simultaneous interpretation remain human translators' domain.[^9] In these areas, AI is an assistive tool, not a replacement. Some adapters chose this path—abandoning technical document translation (which has been largely replaced by MTPE) and moving into high-barrier fields like law, literature, and localization. But the seats on this path are limited—not everyone can find a foothold in these niche markets.

## II. The Illustration Industry: Accelerating Creation with AI Tools

In the illustration industry, the adapter profile differs from that in translation. Translation adapters are "downgrading to survive"—going from creator to reviewer. Illustration adapters are more like "upgrading and accelerating"—using AI tools to double their creative speed.

**The SpeedPainter Phenomenon:**

After 2023, a cohort of concept designers began publicly using Midjourney and Stable Diffusion as part of their workflow. Their approach:

1. Use AI to generate dozens of concept sketches, rapidly exploring visual directions
2. Select the most promising options
3. Refine details in Photoshop or Procreate
4. Deliver the final product

This workflow increased their output speed by 3-5x while actually improving quality—because they spent their time on "selection" and "refinement" rather than "sketching from scratch."[^10]

Zhao Ming, mentioned earlier, is a textbook case: in 2023, he transitioned from freelance illustrator to "AI art director," with income 20% higher than before.[^11] His work shifted from "drawing" to "selecting"—choosing the most client-suitable direction from hundreds of AI-generated candidates, then adjusting details.

**Structural changes in the gaming industry:**

Inside game companies, the art staff who adapted were not the ones被淘汰—they were the ones who stayed. In 2023, approximately 40% of game companies began using AI-generated art,[^12] but these companies didn't fire all their artists. Instead, they reduced junior art positions (those doing repetitive work) while retaining or even expanding senior art positions (those making aesthetic judgments and directing art).

An art director at a mid-sized game company described the change: "Before, my team had 50 people—30 creating assets, 15 doing concept design, 5 setting art direction. Now the team is 20—10 generate assets with AI and refine them, 5 do concept design (assisted by AI), 5 do art direction. AI eliminated the asset-creation positions, but raised the value of everyone else."[^13]

**The illustrator adapter's psychological ledger:**

As in translation, adapters face an identity shift. One concept designer at a game company said: "My colleagues used to call me 'painter.' Now they call me 'AI trainer.' My hands haven't changed, but the nature of the work has completely changed. My income went up, but I'm not sure this is still the career I originally wanted."[^14]

**The core skill shift for adapters:**

From "drawing well" to "selecting accurately." Aesthetic ability—judging what is good, what fits the brief, what has expressiveness—became the scarcest skill. Hand-drawing ability remains important, but its importance shifted from "core competency" to "bonus point."

## III. The Programming Industry: Copilot and the New Definition of "10x Engineer"

In the programming industry, the pattern of AI impact differs starkly from the previous two. In translation and illustration, it's "low-end replaced, high-end survives." In programming, it's "everyone becomes more efficient, but entry-level positions contract."

**GitHub Copilot's productivity data:**

According to GitHub's 2023 report, developers using Copilot completed tasks 55% faster.[^15] But this 55% was not evenly distributed:

- Senior developers: 30%-40% efficiency gain (they were already fast; Copilot accelerated the boilerplate code portions)
- Mid-level developers: 50%-60% efficiency gain (the biggest beneficiary group)
- Junior developers: 20%-30% efficiency gain (they need to understand code before they can judge whether Copilot's suggestions are correct)

What does this mean? A senior developer using Copilot can now handle workloads that previously required 2-3 people. The Silicon Valley myth of "10x engineer" became reality in the AI era—except the "10x" isn't due to extraordinary talent, but to having an AI assistant.

**The adapter pattern:**

In programming, adapters aren't people who "learned AI tools"—they were already good developers; AI merely amplified their abilities. They use Copilot for boilerplate code, use Claude Code for refactoring and debugging, and spend the saved time on architecture design, code review, and technical decisions.

A senior engineer at a tech company described the change: "Before, I wrote 300 lines of effective code a day. Now it's 500 lines. But the bigger change is—before, 60% of my time was spent writing code, 40% thinking about architecture. Now it's reversed. Copilot handles the boring parts, and I can spend more time on things that truly matter."[^16]

**What makes programming adaptation unique:**

Unlike translation and illustration, programming adapters don't need to "downgrade"—their original skills haven't depreciated, they've just been amplified in efficiency. AI hasn't replaced programmers' creativity; it has replaced their repetitive labor. This makes adaptation costs lower in programming than in the previous two industries—a senior developer can learn Copilot in days, while a translator needs months to adapt to MTPE.

**The junior developer dilemma:**

But here's a cruel side effect. When a senior developer can do the work of three, companies' demand for junior developers drops.[^17] In 2023, multiple tech companies reduced junior engineering hiring. This means: programming's "entry ladder" is being broken by AI.

The adapter dividend is partly built on the losses of juniors.

## IV. The Writing Industry: Journalists and Authors with AI-Assisted First Drafts

The writing industry's adaptation pattern is similar to programming—AI is an accelerator, not a replacement. But "adaptation" here is more nuanced, because writing's value lies not only in "producing text" but in "having opinions, style, and judgment."

**Adaptation in journalism:**

Some news organizations began using AI to assist with first-draft writing—particularly data-driven reporting (earnings analysis, sports event summaries, election result coverage).[^18] AI can rapidly turn data into article scaffolding, and journalists add context, interviews, and analysis on top.

BuzzFeed announced in 2023 that it would use AI to generate personalized content (such as travel recommendations) while retaining an editorial team for quality control.[^19] The Associated Press began using AI to automatically generate corporate earnings news as early as 2014, covering thousands of companies by 2023.[^20]

Adapting journalists are not people replaced by AI—they learned to use AI as a "junior reporter." AI handles research and first drafts; humans handle judgment, editing, and in-depth interviews.

But journalism's adaptation has an ethical dimension unlike other industries. When an article is AI-assisted, readers have the right to know. Some media outlets began marking articles with "this article was AI-assisted"—this is not a technical issue; it is a trust issue.[^21] When readers are uncertain whether the "thinker" behind the words is a human or a machine, journalism's authority is shaken.

**Authors' adaptation:**

Some authors began using AI to assist with creation—particularly in outline design, world-building, and character relationship mapping. AI doesn't write the final text, but it helps authors rapidly explore possibilities in the early creative stages.

One web novelist (anonymous) described the workflow change: "I use Claude to help me outline—I can explore five or six story directions in a day, which used to take me a week of solitary brainstorming. Once I pick a direction, the writing itself is still mine. AI can't replace my prose, but it replaced the time I used to spend staring at a blank document."[^22]

This "AI builds the skeleton, humans add the flesh" division of labor is becoming the standard workflow in the content industry. In the field of search engine optimization (SEO), AI-assisted writing has become extremely common—large volumes of technical documentation, product descriptions, and knowledge articles have their first drafts generated by AI, then quality-checked by human editors.[^23]

**The adapter's boundary:**

The writing industry's adaptation has a clear boundary: wherever "original ideas" and "personal style" are required, AI can't help. Investigative reporting, literary creation, opinion pieces—in these areas, AI can only assist with research and initial drafts; the true value remains in human hands.[^24]

## V. Common Characteristics of Adapters

Examining the adapters across all four industries, a clear pattern emerges.

**They learned new tools—but tools aren't the key.** Translators learned MTPE, illustrators learned Midjourney, programmers learned Copilot, journalists learned AI-assisted writing. But learning the tool is merely the threshold. What truly sets adapters apart are the points below.

**They went from "executor" to "editor/decision-maker."** This is the common shift across all adapters. Translators went from "translator" to "reviewer," illustrators from "painter" to "aesthetic decision-maker," programmers from "code writer" to "architecture designer," journalists from "reporter" to "editorial judge."

In every case, AI took over "execution," and human value shifted toward "judgment." The adapters are not the people with the fastest hands—they are the people with the best eye.

**They repositioned their value.** Adapters made a critical psychological shift: no longer treating "I can do this" as core value, but treating "I know what this should look like" as core value. Skills shifted from muscle memory to judgment.

**They were proactive, not reactive.** Zhao Ming began learning AI tools in early 2023—not after his income plummeted, but after seeing the trend.[^24] The common trait of adapters: they started changing before they "had to." By the time income drops 50%, it's too late to learn new tools—because by then the market is already saturated with adapters.

**Their age and seniority distribution is uneven.** Across all four industries, the most successful adapters are "mid-to-senior" practitioners—those with 5-10 years of experience. They have enough domain knowledge to judge AI output quality, enough industry connections to land projects, and enough financial buffer to weather the income dip during transition.

Both newcomers and veterans have lower adaptation rates. Newcomers lack judgment; veterans lack motivation (or lack digital literacy).

**They paid hidden costs.** Income decline is the visible cost—everyone can see it. But hidden costs deserve more attention: identity rupture ("I'm no longer a painter"), loss of professional fulfillment ("I've become an AI operator"), and uncertainty about the future ("will the next AI upgrade make reviewers unnecessary too?"). Adapters kept their jobs, but didn't necessarily keep their dignity.

**The gap between them is widening.** Adaptation is not happening uniformly. Within the same translation company, the income gap between translators who can do MTPE and those who can't is growing. Within the same game company, the job security of concept designers who use AI tools and those who can only hand-draw is diverging. Adaptation has created a new stratification—the chasm between "those who can use AI" and "those who can't" is becoming a new class line.

## Assessment

Adaptation is not surrender—it is a new skill.

This sentence deserves careful chewing. On the surface, adapters are "realists"—they saw AI coming, learned AI tools, and kept working. But on a deeper level, what adapters did was far more difficult than "learning tools": they redefined their professional identity.

A translator going from "I am a translator" to "I am a translation quality gatekeeper" is not just a skill switch—it is a reconstruction of self-concept. A decade of professional training, accumulated industry reputation, and established client relationships all need to be re-understood within this new framework. This process is painful; it requires a person to admit: the core skills I once took pride in are no longer what the market values most.

But here's the question: **can everyone adapt?**

The answer is no. Adaptation requires three conditions: time, resources, and judgment. Time—learning new tools and workflows takes months to a year. Resources—during the transition period of declining income, you need savings or other income to sustain yourself. Judgment—you need to accurately assess industry trends, neither clinging to old patterns nor blindly following the crowd.

These three conditions exclude the most vulnerable groups: newcomers (lacking industry judgment and financial buffers), older practitioners (higher transition costs), and freelancers and gig workers (without company-provided training and transition periods).

The deeper question is: **who bears the cost of adaptation?**

The new skills adapters learned did help them keep their jobs, but their incomes generally declined. MTPE's per-thousand-word rate is 50%-70% that of pure human translation. Illustrators' "AI-assisted output" quotes are far below pure hand-drawn rates. Adaptation kept the rice bowl, but shrank it.

Meanwhile, where did the efficiency dividends from adaptation flow? To clients and platforms. Translation companies used MTPE to cut costs but didn't share the savings with the review translators. Game companies used AI to shrink art teams and converted the savings into profits.

Adapters preserved themselves—but they didn't preserve the industry's average income level. When everyone adapts and everyone accepts lower prices, "adaptation" is no longer an advantage but a new baseline.

This is the cruelest part: adaptation is not an endpoint; it's merely a new starting line. On this new starting line, competition still exists—only the dimensions have shifted from "who draws better" to "whose aesthetic judgment is sharper," from "who translates faster" to "whose review efficiency is higher."

Adapters won this round. But the next round?

AI's evolution won't stop at 2024. When GPT-5 or the next generation of models takes translation quality up another notch, will MTPE still need human reviewers? When AI-generated images no longer require human refinement, what will concept designers do? When AI can write its own tests and debug its own code, how much advantage will senior developers have left?

Adaptation is not a one-time victory—it is a continuous race. Today's adapters may be tomorrow's被淘汰者. In the era of accelerating AI evolution, the "shelf life" of adaptation is growing shorter.

Perhaps the most honest answer is: no one knows what adaptation's endgame looks like. All we know is—those who stop will surely lose; those who keep running still have a chance.

But "running" requires conditions. Not everyone has the stamina to run, the sense of direction to run, or the financial support to run. The story of adaptation deserves to be recorded—because it demonstrates human resilience in the face of technological change. But the story of adaptation also needs to be questioned—because when "adaptation" becomes an obligation, what happens to those who can't run?

This is the question *Transformation: Who Adapted* most wants to leave behind. Adapters' courage deserves respect, but a system that only rewards runners and doesn't care for those who can't walk is not a good system.
