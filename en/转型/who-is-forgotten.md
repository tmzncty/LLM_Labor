# Who Is Forgotten

> In the wave of AI, some stories have been recorded—the suffering of Kenyan annotators, the Hollywood writers' strikes, the anger of illustrators. But there are many more whose stories have not been recorded. They are quietly replaced, quietly pivot careers, quietly disappear into the statistics. They are the "silent majority" of AI's impact.

## I. The Forgotten Annotators

Annotators are the most forgotten group in the AI industry chain.

Their work is invisible—bound by non-disclosure agreements, unable to reveal their work content.[^1] Their health is neglected—no counseling, no health insurance, no long-term support.[^2] Their voices are suppressed—no unions, no collective bargaining rights, no political representation.[^3]

A typical annotator's story:

**Annotator F** (pseudonym, Kenya, resigned):

> "I worked at Sama for 8 months. Every day I read violent, pornographic, and hateful content. I started having nightmares, started dreading going to work. I went to the doctor, who said I had PTSD. The company provided no help. I could only resign."

> "After I resigned, I had no income. I couldn't tell anyone why I resigned—because I had signed a non-disclosure agreement. My family didn't know what I had been through. I could only carry it alone."

> "Now, I work as a cashier at a supermarket. The pay is less than the annotation work, but at least I don't have to look at those horrible things every day."

This story appeared in no news report. This annotator's name appeared in no company annual report. Their PTSD was recorded in no statistics.

They are a "silent one" of AI's impact.

## II. The Forgotten Translators

The impact on the translation industry has been widely reported. But what's been reported is "industry trends"—market shrinkage, declining income, MTPE replacing pure human translation.

What's been forgotten are **individual stories.**

**Freelance translator Zhang Ming** (pseudonym):

> "I did translation for 15 years. In 2023, my income dropped from 250,000 yuan to 150,000 yuan. In 2024, it dropped further to 100,000 yuan. The translation company said, 'The market has changed; you need to adapt.' How was I supposed to adapt? I'm already 40 years old. Translation is all I know."

> "I sent out over 100 resumes. Most had no reply. Some replies said, 'We need someone who can use AI tools.' I never learned AI tools in school. I'm 40 years old—I'm not sure I can learn new things anymore."

> "Now, I work in administration at a foreign trade company. The salary is 6,000 yuan/month. Less than half of what I earned as a translator. But at least I have a job."

This translator's story will not appear in any industry report. The industry report says "translation industry income declined 30%," but it won't say "Zhang Ming's income declined 60%."

## III. The Forgotten Customer Service Agents

Klarna replaced 700 customer service agents with AI.[^4] This news was widely reported.

But what was forgotten is where those 700 people **went.**

**Customer service agent Li Hua** (pseudonym, laid off by Klarna):

> "I worked as a customer service agent at Klarna for 3 years. In 2024, the company announced that AI customer service could handle 70% of conversations. We were told the company was 'optimizing team structure.' I was laid off."

> "Klarna gave me 3 months of severance pay. After 3 months, I had no income. I sent out over 200 resumes and received only 10 interview invitations. Most customer service positions require 'ability to use AI tools.' I never learned AI tools in school."

> "Now, I work as a restaurant server. The pay is less than the customer service job. But at least I have a job."

Klarna's press release said "AI customer service improved efficiency." But it didn't say "Li Hua went from customer service agent to restaurant server."

## IV. The Forgotten Programmers

The impact on junior programmers has been discussed extensively. GitHub Copilot can generate 60-70% of boilerplate code.[^5] Companies' demand for junior programmers is declining.

But what's been forgotten are **those who are currently learning to program.**

**Computer science student Wang Ming** (pseudonym):

> "I'm a junior, studying programming. In 2023, GitHub Copilot came out. I saw news saying 'AI will replace junior programmers.' I started wondering: do I still need to learn programming? If AI can write code, what's the point of spending 4 years studying computer science?"

> "I asked my teacher. My teacher said, 'AI is just a tool—programmers are still the decision-makers.' But when I saw tech companies laying people off, I stopped believing that."

> "I don't know what to do. I'm already a junior—I can't change my major. I can only pray that when I graduate, the market will still need programmers."

This student's story will not appear in any news report. The news report says "AI will affect employment," but it won't say "Wang Ming doesn't know whether he should keep studying programming."

## V. The Forgotten Artists

The illustrators' anger has been recorded. The ArtStation "NO AI" protests, copyright lawsuits, the Glaze and Nightshade tools.[^6]

But what's been forgotten are the artists who **didn't participate in the protests.**

**Freelance illustrator Chen Ming** (pseudonym):

> "I'm not a well-known artist. I have 200 followers on ArtStation. I make my living drawing illustrations—concept art for game companies, cover art for publishers, illustrations for ad agencies."

> "In 2023, my orders dropped by half. Game companies started using Midjourney for concept art. Publishers started using AI to generate covers. Ad agencies started using AI for illustrations."

> "I couldn't participate in the protests—I needed to work. I couldn't sue AI companies—I didn't have the money. I could only accept reality and figure out how to adapt."

> "Now, I'm learning how to use AI tools. I use Midjourney to generate initial drafts, then manually revise. Efficiency went up, but income didn't—because clients know AI can do most of the work, and they're not willing to pay a premium for manual revisions."

This illustrator's story will not appear in media reports. The media reports on "illustrators' anger," but it won't say "Chen Ming quietly accepted reality."

## VI. The Forgotten Families

AI's impact doesn't just affect individuals—it affects entire families.

**An annotator's wife** (anonymous, Kenya):

> "My husband used to do annotation work at Sama. After he resigned, our family's income was cut in half. I didn't know why he resigned—he wouldn't tell me. Later, I heard from a neighbor that annotation work requires reading 'horrible things.'"

> "Now, he works as a loader at a factory. The pay is less than before. Our children's school fees have become a problem. But we have no choice—we can only accept it."

This family's story will not appear in any news report. The news report says "AI has impacted employment," but it won't say "an annotator's children may not be able to afford school."

## VII. The Forgotten Communities

AI's impact doesn't just affect individuals and families—it affects entire communities.

**Filipino annotator communities:**

Some cities in the Philippines—such as certain districts in Manila—are heavily dependent on annotation work.[^7] When annotation work shifts to other countries, these communities' economies are severely impacted.

But this community-level impact is recorded in no statistics. The statistics say "the number of Filipino annotators declined," but they won't say "consumer spending in a certain Manila district dropped 30%."

## VIII. Who Will Record Them?

These forgotten people—annotators, translators, customer service agents, programmers, artists, their families, their communities—who will record their stories?

Mainstream media will not record their stories. Media needs "big news"—major company layoffs, large-scale protests, shocking statistics. Individual stories aren't "big" enough, aren't "news" enough.

Academic research will not record their stories. Academic research needs "data"—surveys, statistical analysis, model validation. Individual stories aren't "quantitative" enough, aren't "academic" enough.

AI companies will not record their stories. AI companies need "positive narratives"—AI improves efficiency, AI creates value, AI changes the world. Displaced workers are not part of the "positive narrative."

**Only we ourselves can record these stories.**

This is the significance of the LLM_Labor project. Not to write "big news," not to do "academic research," not to create "positive narratives"—but to record the forgotten, so that their stories are not swallowed by history.

## Assessment

The forgotten are not a minority.

According to Anthropic CEO's projections, AI could eliminate up to 50% of entry-level white-collar jobs within 1-5 years.[^8] This means tens of millions of people will be affected. Most of their stories will not be recorded—they will quietly disappear into the statistics.

Statistics are cold. "Translation industry income declined 30%"—what does that mean? It means tens of thousands of translators' incomes dropped dramatically, their families' living standards declined, and their children may have lost access to better education.

"Klarna replaced 700 customer service agents with AI"—what does that mean? It means 700 people lost their jobs, 700 families lost their income sources, and 700 people need to find their footing again.

These "meanings" do not appear in news reports. The news report says "AI improved efficiency," but it won't say "700 people lost their jobs."

The LLM_Labor project's mission is to record these forgotten stories. Not to manufacture fear, not to obstruct technological progress—but to make these stories heard.

Technological progress is not neutral. It has winners and losers. The winners' stories have been recorded—Silicon Valley valuations, AI breakthroughs, efficiency gains. But the losers' stories have been forgotten—annotators' PTSD, translators' unemployment, customer service agents' career changes.

If we only record the winners' stories, our history is incomplete. If we only see technology's progress, we cannot see technology's costs.

The forgotten deserve to be recorded. Their stories deserve to be heard. Their suffering deserves to be acknowledged.
