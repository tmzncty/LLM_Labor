# Who Embraces AI

> While artists collectively protest the use of their work in AI training data, others are enthusiastically embracing this transformation. Refik Anadol's massive AI-generated data sculptures are exhibited at the Museum of Modern Art in New York; musician Holly Herndon trained her own AI alter ego "Holly+" and invited other musicians to create with her voice. This is not the eccentricity of a minority—it is an emerging trend. In this AI transformation, those who embrace AI deserve the same rigorous scrutiny as those who resist.

## I. The Rise of AI Artists

Refik Anadol's studio is based in Los Angeles. His work draws on large-scale datasets and machine learning algorithms to generate flowing, immersive visual installations. His *Unsupervised* (2022) was exhibited at MoMA, using over 200 years of artwork data from the museum's collection to train algorithms that generate ever-changing abstract images.[^1]

The Turkish-born artist's attitude toward AI can be summarized as: tools don't matter; intention is the soul of art. He has said: "I've never worried about AI replacing artists—AI has no desire, no pain, no childhood memories."[^2]

Musician Holly Herndon went further. In 2021, she released the "Holly+" project—an AI model trained on her own voice. Anyone can upload a melody and receive a version "sung" by her. She even established a decentralized autonomous organization (DAO) for this AI alter ego, allowing the community to collectively manage its usage rules.[^3]

Herndon's logic: rather than letting someone else train an AI on my voice, I'd rather do it myself and set the rules.

## II. New Work Models of Human-Machine Collaboration

Beyond the creative industries, "human-machine collaboration" is emerging as a new work paradigm.

**Screenwriters and AI**: Hollywood screenwriter John August (screenwriter of *Big Fish*) publicly stated during the 2023 writers' strike that he uses AI to generate "garbage drafts" of screenplays, which he then rewrites. He believes AI helps him skip the most painful "blank page phase."

**Translators and machines**: The translation industry has already undergone a similar transformation. Veteran translator Wu Gang (pseudonym) says: "Ten years ago I opposed machine translation; now I can't live without it. MTPE (Machine Translation Post-Editing) is three to five times more efficient than pure human translation. The problem isn't that AI arrived—it's that clients use it to drive down prices."[^4]

**Designers' "AI assistant"**: Graphic designer Sarah Chen has high ratings on Fiverr. She integrated Midjourney into her workflow: "I can now do the work of three people by myself. But my rates have dropped too—clients know you use AI and think it should be cheaper."

## III. Success Stories and Failure Stories

**Successes: Finding irreplaceability:**

- **Photographer Platon**: Known for portraits of political leaders, Platon announced in 2023 that he uses AI for post-processing, but emphasized that "that moment of eye contact" cannot be replicated by AI. He treats AI as an upgraded version of Photoshop, not a threat.
- **Illustrator Ariel Sinha**: She trained a LoRA model based on her own style and batch-generates variations, but still hand-adjusts every piece's details. She says: "AI is my intern, not my replacement."

**Failures: Devoured by the algorithm:**

- **The collapse of content farms**: In 2023, multiple websites that relied on AI to mass-produce content were penalized by Google's algorithms. These sites had pitched "100 AI-written articles a day" as their selling point, only to nearly vanish in search engine updates.[^5]
- **The predicament of AI music**: Independent musician Mark used AI tools to release 200 songs in two months, but Spotify's algorithmic recommendation system identified his content as "low-quality mass production" and reduced the recommendation weight of all his songs. He said: "I embraced AI too thoroughly, and the platform abandoned me instead."[^5]

## IV. The Merits and Limits of "New Tool Theory"

Artists and workers who support AI often invoke a perspective: AI is just a tool, like the camera, Photoshop, or the electronic synthesizer. Every time a new technology appears, people panic, but ultimately artists adapt and find new modes of expression.

This "new tool theory" has its merits:

1. **Historical precedent**: 19th-century painters were indeed panicked by cameras, but photography ultimately developed into an independent art form, and painting did not die.
2. **Efficiency gains**: For certain tasks (such as first drafts, variations, style transfer), AI can significantly reduce repetitive labor.
3. **Democratization potential**: AI lowers the technical barriers for certain creative activities, enabling more people to participate in creation.

But "new tool theory" has its limits:

1. **Tools differ from tools**: A camera doesn't mimic a specific painter's style; Photoshop doesn't train on photographers' works. AI's "tool nature" differs fundamentally from traditional tools—it absorbs other creators' labor.[^6]
2. **Power asymmetry**: The companies developing AI models reap disproportionate gains, while creators who contributed training data receive virtually no compensation. This isn't a "tool" problem—it's a distribution problem.
3. **Differences in substitutability**: A camera replaced painting's function of "recording reality," but didn't replace painting itself. AI may directly replace certain creative processes—such as commercial illustration, background music, and copywriting.

## V. Embrace vs. Resist: Personal Choice or Structural Problem?

When discussing AI, we often reduce it to personal choice: you can embrace it or resist it. But this framework ignores a crucial question—choices themselves are shaped by structures.

**The gaming industry example**: Game artist Li Ming (pseudonym) says: "Of course I want to resist AI, but the company has already purchased an enterprise license for Midjourney. If I don't use it and my colleagues do, I'll be 'optimized.'" His "choice" is between unemployment and embracing AI—this is not genuine free choice.

**The freelancer's dilemma**: Freelance illustrator Xiaoyu once resisted AI, until her clients started disappearing. "Clients bring AI-generated images to me and say 'just make some adjustments based on this.' If I refuse, they find someone else." She ultimately chose to learn AI tools, but describes the feeling as "a dignified surrender."[^7]

**Structural factors:**

1. **Information asymmetry**: Large tech companies understand AI's capability boundaries far better than ordinary workers, enabling them to make strategic adjustments earlier.
2. **Bargaining power disparity**: Silicon Valley engineers can demand that their companies not use AI; freelancers and gig workers have no such bargaining power.
3. **Time lag**: Resisting AI requires time to build alternative income sources, but market changes don't wait.

## Assessment

Is embracing AI "adaptation" or "surrender"?

This question has no simple answer. History will remember those who resisted AI, but it should not forget the complex circumstances of those who embraced it. They are not simply "traitors" or "opportunists"—they are rational actors caught in a bind.

**The "adaptation" narrative**: Embracing AI is keeping pace with the times, just as print workers learned to operate printing presses and photographers learned to use Photoshop. Technological change is irreversible; early adapters gain competitive advantage.

**The "surrender" narrative**: Embracing AI is a forced compromise in a game with asymmetric power. When an entire industry is using AI, an individual's "choice" is merely an illusion. True choice lies in the hands of those who design the algorithms, control the platforms, and set the rules.

Both narratives have merit, but neither is complete.

A more accurate description might be: **embracing AI is a form of "bounded rationality" adaptation.** Under given constraints, individuals make optimal choices, but those constraints are themselves unjust. A photographer's choice to use AI may be rational, but if training data was used without consent, this "rationality" is built on injustice.

Holly Herndon's "Holly+" project offers an interesting counterpoint: she proactively controlled the AI model's training process, established community governance mechanisms, and retained veto power over voice usage. This may be a more just form of embracing—but it requires technical capability, capital accumulation, and community influence, conditions that most ordinary workers lack.

Therefore, the real question is not "should we embrace AI," but: **under what conditions is embracing AI a just choice?** If training data is compensated, if AI tools' benefits are more equitably distributed, if workers have genuine exit options—then embracing AI is not surrender, but partnership. But until these conditions are met, "embracing AI" is closer to a rational compromise made under unjust rules of the game.
