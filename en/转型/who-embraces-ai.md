# Who Is Embracing AI: Adoption Is Mostly Augmentation So Far

> **As of 2026-09-21. Status: REVISED.** “Embracing AI” is first an adoption question: who actually uses the technology, for which tasks, and whether use is accompanied by employment changes. New 2026 evidence requires one more distinction: **theoretical occupational or task exposure to AI is not the same thing as observed worker adoption.**

Using the 2026 US Census BTOS AI supplement, NBER researchers find that 18% of firms used AI in at least one function during Nov 2025–Jan 2026 (32% employment-weighted). Adoption is concentrated in large, knowledge-intensive firms but usually remains narrow: 57% of adopting firms use AI in three or fewer functions and 65% in three or fewer worker tasks.[^1]

Among adopters, 66% report using AI for task augmentation; only about 2% report employment reductions. That does not establish that future employment effects will remain small. It does show that current adoption cannot simply be translated into headcount reduction.[^1]

Worker use can also occur without a formal firm-wide adoption program. Earlier national survey evidence found 23% of employed US respondents had used generative AI for work in the previous week by late 2024.[^2]

A newer St. Louis Fed/NBER study builds occupation- and task-level adoption indexes from four waves of the Real-Time Population Survey between August 2025 and May 2026, covering nearly 14,000 workers. By May 2026, about 45% of workers reported using generative AI for their jobs.[^3] This is worker-reported use, not evidence that 45% of firms had formally deployed AI.

## Exposure is not adoption

The new task-level data provide an important counterweight to exposure rankings. AI use is already broad: more than 80% of occupations have at least 20% worker adoption, and more than 40% of tasks have at least 20% adoption. But use remains shallow: only about 40% of occupations have adoption above 50%, and fewer than 3% of tasks are performed with AI by at least half of the workers who do them.[^3]

Some manual or face-to-face occupations adopt far more AI than common exposure measures predict because they also contain information-gathering, planning and communication tasks. The study highlights computer and office-machine repairers, special-education teachers, and laundry and dry-cleaning workers as examples.[^3]

Exposure measures still do reasonably well at ranking occupations or tasks by likely adoption, but they are poor predictors of whether a particular worker adopts AI. Within most occupations and tasks, some workers adopt and many do not. Workers with at least six months of generative-AI experience also use it across more tasks, suggesting that learning and experimentation costs matter.[^3]

At least four stages therefore need to remain separate:

- whether a model is capable of performing a task;
- whether an occupation or task is theoretically exposed;
- whether workers actually use AI;
- whether firms formally reorganize workflows, staffing, evaluation or management around it.

The Real-Time Population Survey evidence is US self-reported use. It does not measure net productivity, firm-wide deployment, job destruction or job creation. Its value is precisely that it prevents “AI can do this” from being rewritten as “firms already use it,” “the job has already changed,” or “the worker has already been displaced.”

Meta's failed 2026 Project OT remains an important organizational counterexample: aggressive AI-native reorganization can stall because of worker resistance, poor reliability, security problems and disappointing productivity.[^4]

“Embracing AI” should therefore be documented as organizational experimentation rather than a synonym for successful automation. The new adoption evidence strengthens a broader rule for the repository: **capability → exposure → adoption → organizational redesign → employment outcome** are separate transitions that require separate evidence.
