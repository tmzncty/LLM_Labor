# Junior Programmers: The Occupation Is Not Disappearing, but the Entry Door Is Narrowing

> **As of 2026-08-28. v2 REVISED.** Unsupported legacy claims about universal hiring declines, code-generation percentages, and multi-fold productivity gains have been removed, along with fabricated worker interviews.

## Separate software-development employment from junior entry

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics currently projects employment of Software Developers to grow about 10% from 2025 to 2035, from roughly 1.718 million to 1.893 million. It also expects about 106,100 annual openings across developers, QA analysts, and testers. BLS lists demand for AI, automation, robotics, and related software as one source of growth.[^1]

So “AI can write code” does not establish that software-development employment as a whole is collapsing.

The entry-level pipeline can still weaken at the same time.

## Evidence of a young-worker divergence

Stanford Digital Economy Lab's August 2026 update using ADP payroll data found weaker employment growth for workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations. By June 2026 the relative-path gap was about 19% compared with similarly aged workers in less-exposed occupations.[^2]

Software development is among the highly exposed occupations discussed in the research. The adjustment appears more strongly through reduced hiring than through unusually high separations.

The 19% figure is **not** an absolute junior-programmer job-loss rate, and the researchers do not attribute every difference to AI.

## Why junior tasks are exposed

Many traditional learning tasks—boilerplate code, simple CRUD, first-pass tests, documentation, small migrations, routine debugging—are precisely the activities coding assistants can help experienced developers complete faster or with less delegation.

A firm may therefore need fewer junior hands today while unintentionally weakening the pipeline from which future senior engineers are trained.

## AI does not always improve experienced-developer productivity

METR ran a randomized controlled study in 2025 with 16 experienced open-source developers completing 246 real tasks in repositories they knew well. Under those specific conditions, allowing early-2025 AI tools made tasks take about 19% longer on average.[^3]

The study is narrow and should not be generalized to all software work. It is nevertheless important counterevidence against the assumption that coding agents automatically create large productivity gains in every setting.

## What we know

- coding tools can perform meaningful portions of routine development work;
- young workers in highly AI-exposed occupations show a weaker hiring/employment trajectory in U.S. payroll data;
- software-development employment overall is still projected to grow;
- productivity effects vary substantially by task, codebase, expertise, and tool generation.

## What we do not know

- technology-sector hiring weakness since 2022 cannot all be attributed to AI;
- there is no reliable universal percentage decline in junior-programmer jobs;
- 2025 tool studies will not permanently represent later systems;
- total occupation growth does not guarantee a healthy entry-level pipeline;
- productivity gains may become higher output, lower prices, more demand, or lower headcount depending on organizational choices.

## Analysis

The central contradiction is temporal: a senior developer with AI may absorb tasks once delegated to a junior worker, making the organization more efficient today. But tomorrow's senior developers were supposed to grow out of today's junior cohort.

Software development can expand as an occupation while its traditional route of entry is rewritten.
