# Medical Diagnostics

> The medical industry is considered one of the fields "hardest for AI to replace"—requiring professional knowledge, interpersonal communication, ethical judgment, and licenses. But AI is quietly changing the形态 of medicine. When AI can read X-rays more accurately than radiologists, when AI can diagnose skin diseases more accurately than dermatologists, when AI can provide preliminary diagnoses faster than general practitioners—the medical industry faces not "complete replacement" but "structural reorganization."

## I. Before the Impact: What This Profession Used to Be

Doctors are among the most respected professions in society. From medical school to residency to attending physician, training a doctor typically takes 10-15 years.

**Medical Industry Size**:
- In 2023, global healthcare expenditure was approximately $9 trillion[^1]
- U.S. healthcare expenditure was approximately $4.5 trillion, accounting for 17% of GDP[^2]
- China's healthcare expenditure was approximately 8 trillion RMB[^3]

**Doctor Income Levels**:
- United States: Specialist doctors $300,000-$500,000 annually, general practitioners $200,000-$250,000[^4]
- China: Chief physicians at top-tier hospitals 300,000-800,000 RMB annually, resident physicians 100,000-200,000 RMB[^5]

**What Doctors Do**:
- Diagnosis: Determining diseases through symptoms and test results
- Treatment: Developing treatment plans, prescribing medication, performing surgery
- Prevention: Health screenings, vaccinations, health education
- Communication: Communicating with patients and families, explaining conditions

## II. The Impact: What AI Did

AI applications in healthcare are expanding from "auxiliary tools" to "replacing部分 diagnostic functions."

**Imaging Diagnosis**:

AI's performance in imaging diagnosis has surpassed human doctors.[^6]

- **Radiology**: AI can detect lung cancer, breast cancer, fractures, etc., with accuracy equal to or exceeding radiologists
- **Dermatology**: AI can diagnose skin cancer from photos with accuracy comparable to dermatologists
- **Ophthalmology**: AI can detect diabetic retinopathy from fundus photos, FDA-approved
- **Pathology**: AI can analyze tissue samples to detect cancer cells

**Drug Discovery**:

AI is accelerating the drug discovery process.[^7]
- DeepMind's AlphaFold has predicted the structure of几乎所有 known proteins
- Insilico Medicine used AI to discover a new drug in 18 months (traditional methods take 4-5 years)
- Major pharmaceutical companies (Pfizer, Novartis, Roche) are deploying AI on a large scale

**Clinical Decision Support**:

AI can help doctors make better decisions:[^8]
- Analyzing patient electronic health records to identify risk factors
- Recommending treatment plans based on latest research evidence
- Predicting disease progression and treatment outcomes
- Monitoring patient vital signs for timely alerts

## V. Response: Who Adapted, Who Left

**AI as an Augmentation Tool**:

Successful medical AI deployments are typically "augmentation" rather than "replacement":
- AI does initial screening, doctors make final diagnosis
- AI provides reference suggestions, doctors make decisions
- AI handles data, doctors handle patients

**Fields Still Requiring Humans**:

- **Complex Diagnosis**: Requires synthesizing multiple types of information and considering patient-specific circumstances[^12]
- **Surgical Operations**: Requires hand-eye coordination, improvisation, and精细 operations[^13]
- **Patient Communication**: Requires emotional connection, empathy, and trust building[^14]
- **Ethical Decision-Making**: Requires weighing pros and cons, respecting patient wishes, and considering social impact[^15]

**Regulatory Progress**:

- The U.S. FDA has approved hundreds of AI medical devices[^16]
- The EU is developing regulatory frameworks for AI medical devices[^17]
- China's NMPA has approved multiple AI medical devices[^18]

## Assessment

The AI impact on the medical industry has a fundamental difference from industries like translation and illustration: **the medical industry has stricter regulations, higher professional thresholds, and more complex ethical considerations.**

Translation and illustration are "low-threshold, low-regulation" industries—anyone can do translation, anyone can draw. But medicine is a "high-threshold, high-regulation" industry—you need licenses, training, and responsibility. This high threshold provides doctors with some protection—AI cannot prescribe medication, perform surgery, or sign diagnostic reports.

But the scope of this protection is shrinking.

AI's performance in imaging diagnosis has already surpassed human doctors. When AI can read X-rays more accurately than radiologists, how much market value does a radiologist's "professional judgment" still have? When AI can diagnose skin diseases faster than dermatologists, how irreplaceable is a dermatologist's "clinical experience"?

The medical industry is experiencing a "de-skilling"—not complete replacement, but "functional replacement." AI won't replace radiologists, but it will replace radiologists' "image reading" function. AI won't replace dermatologists, but it will replace dermatologists' "initial diagnosis" function.

When AI does 80% of diagnostic work, the doctor's value shifts from "diagnosis" to "review." This shift affects not only income but also professional identity. A radiologist spends 10 years learning to read images—when AI can do most of the work, is their expertise still valuable?

The deeper question is: when AI participates in medical diagnosis, **accountability** becomes blurred. If an AI-suggested diagnosis is wrong, who is responsible? The AI developer, the doctor using the AI, or the hospital reviewing AI output?

The current answer is: doctors bear最终 responsibility. But this answer is being challenged. When AI does 80% of the work and doctors only do 20% of the review, is it fair for doctors to bear 100% of the responsibility?

The future of the medical industry may be: AI handles most standardized diagnostic work, while doctors handle complex, non-standardized cases requiring interpersonal communication. This division of labor may improve efficiency, but it may also create a "dual-track system"—AI handles simple, standardized cases (for low-to-middle-income patients), while doctors handle complex, personalized cases (for high-income patients).

This is not the progress of medicine. This is the differentiation of medicine.
