# Medical Diagnosis: AI Is Changing Reading and Decision Workflows, but “AI Has Surpassed Doctors” Is Not a Sound Summary

> **As of 2026-08-28. v2 REVISED.** This is a high-stakes topic. The previous article generalized selected benchmarks into claims that AI had surpassed radiologists and dermatologists and included unverifiable patient/doctor stories. Those claims have been removed.

## Deployment is real

The U.S. FDA maintains a list of AI-enabled medical devices that have satisfied applicable premarket requirements for their intended uses. A large share are imaging-related.[^1]

Authorization establishes that products have entered regulated medical use. It does not mean the FDA has certified that “AI is more accurate than doctors.”

## Generative AI does not consistently outperform expert physicians

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in *npj Digital Medicine* included 83 studies published between 2018 and 2024. It estimated overall diagnostic accuracy for generative AI at about 52.1% (95% CI 47.0%–57.1%).[^2]

The analysis found no statistically significant difference versus physicians overall or non-expert physicians, but generative AI was significantly worse than **expert physicians** by about 15.8 percentage points. Around 76% of included studies were judged at high risk of bias.[^2]

Individual tasks can still show models matching or outperforming particular comparator groups. The meta-analysis is a warning against turning those task-specific results into “AI has surpassed doctors” as a profession-wide claim.

## A more mature effect is workload redesign

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of radiology workflow found that concurrent AI assistance was associated in some settings with an average reduction of about 27.2% in reading time, while prescreening and second-reader designs could reduce the volume requiring direct human review.[^3]

That is a labor-process effect, not evidence that radiologists can be removed. AI can change which cases physicians see first, how many they can process, and where review time is concentrated.

## Human oversight can fail

A JAMA randomized clinical-vignette study found that standard AI assistance could improve some diagnostic decisions, while systematically biased AI reduced physicians' diagnostic accuracy. The tested image explanations did not fully protect clinicians from the biased system.[^4]

“Human in the loop” is therefore not a guarantee. Human reviewers can be misled by automation.

## Why medical work is not a single diagnostic benchmark

Clinical work also includes history, differential diagnosis, test selection, treatment trade-offs, consent, communication, follow-up, and legal accountability. Model performance on an image or vignette captures only a portion of that work.

## What we do not know

- benchmark accuracy does not equal real-world clinical effectiveness;
- performance cannot be generalized across diseases, devices, and patient populations;
- the evidence base is heterogeneous and often biased;
- time savings may increase throughput rather than reduce physician headcount;
- responsibility among developers, hospitals, and clinicians continues to evolve.

## Analysis

The useful labor question is not “AI or doctor?” It is how much of a clinical workflow is preprocessed by machines, how often humans overturn them, how errors change workload, and who remains accountable for the final decision.
