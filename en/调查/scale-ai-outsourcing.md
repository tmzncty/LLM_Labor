# Scale AI Outsourcing System

> Scale AI is the AI industry's largest data labeling outsourcer. It doesn't train models or make products—it sells "labeled data." Its business model is built upon a global low-wage labor force: Remotasks (Scale AI's crowdsourcing platform) recruits labelers in the Philippines, Venezuela, Kenya, and other countries at $1–3 per hour. While Silicon Valley valuations soar, those who provide the "fuel" for AI struggle at the poverty line.

## I. Background

In 2016, 19-year-old Alexandr Wang (亚历山大·王) dropped out of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and founded Scale AI.[^1] His insight was simple: AI models require massive amounts of labeled data for training, and labeling data is tedious, time-consuming work that requires extensive human labor. Silicon Valley companies didn't want to do this themselves—the cost was too high, and it wasn't their core business.

Scale AI's business model was: providing "data labeling as a service" for AI companies. They didn't train models or make products; they did only one thing—turn raw data into labeled data.[^2] This sounded simple, but Scale AI perfected it.

By 2024, Scale AI's valuation reached $14 billion.[^3] Its client list reads like a "who's who of the AI industry": OpenAI, Meta, Microsoft, Google, the US Department of Defense.[^4] Nearly every major AI model has used data labeled by Scale AI.

Scale AI's success was built on a simple economic principle: labor in developed countries is too expensive, while labor in developing countries is cheap. Alexandr Wang found a way to outsource the most tedious, repetitive work to the world's cheapest labor force.

## II. Core Facts

**Scale AI's Business Structure**:

Scale AI operates two main platforms:
1. **Scale Data Engine**: Premium data labeling service for enterprise clients, primarily used in specialized fields like autonomous driving and medical imaging
2. **Remotasks**: Low-end labeling platform for global crowdworkers, primarily used for basic tasks like text labeling and image classification[^5]

Remotasks is Scale AI's "secret weapon." It's a global crowdsourcing platform that allows anyone to register as a labeler and earn payment for completing tasks.[^6] But unlike established crowdsourcing platforms like Amazon Mechanical Turk, Remotasks is specifically designed for developing countries, with pay levels far below US standards.

**How Remotasks Works**:

1. **Registration**: Anyone with a computer and internet connection can register a Remotasks account
2. **Training**: New users must complete a series of training tasks, learning how to label different types of data
3. **Task Selection**: After training, users can select tasks from the task pool, including image classification, text labeling, voice transcription, etc.
4. **Submission**: After completing tasks, submit results and await review
5. **Payment**: After review approval, payment is deposited into the user's Remotasks account and can be withdrawn to PayPal or a bank account[^7]

**Pay Structure**:

Remotasks pays per task, not per hour. Depending on task type and difficulty, each task pays between $0.01 and $1.[^8] A skilled labeler can complete 10–30 tasks per hour, earning approximately $1–3 per hour.[^9]

This pay level is far below the US federal minimum wage ($7.25/hour), but in the Philippines, Venezuela, Kenya, and other countries, it's already 2–5 times the local minimum wage.[^10]

**Recruitment Methods**:

Remotasks uses various recruitment methods:
- **Social Media Advertising**: Running ads on Facebook and Instagram promoting "work from home, earn easily"
- **Local Agents**: Establishing local agents in the Philippines, Kenya, and other countries to recruit labelers through offline events
- **Word of Mouth**: Existing users referring new users, with both parties receiving rewards[^11]

Remotasks ads typically emphasize "flexibility" and "freedom"—you can work anytime, anywhere, without time or location restrictions. But few mention that this "freedom" comes at the cost of: no minimum wage guarantee, no health insurance, no employment contract.[^12]

## III. Testimonies

**Labeler F** (pseudonym, Philippines, still working):
> "I've been working on Remotasks for two years. At first, I thought it was a good job—I could work from home, the hours were flexible, and the income was above the local minimum wage. But then I realized my income completely depended on what tasks were available in the task pool. Some days I could find enough tasks; other days I couldn't find any at all."[^13]

**Labeler G** (pseudonym, Venezuela, resigned):
> "I'm an engineer, but in Venezuela, an engineer's salary is no longer enough to support a family. I started doing labeling on Remotasks, earning $100–200 per month. This was more than any job I could find locally. But I also knew that this $100–200 was a fraction of what Silicon Valley companies pay an engineer."[^14]

**Labeler H** (pseudonym, Kenya, still working):
> "I work 8–10 hours per day, earning $1.5–2 per hour. This is already a decent income locally. But I also know that in the United States, the same work would pay at least $10 per hour. We're not lazy; we were just born in the wrong place."[^15]

**Former Remotasks Trainer** (anonymous):
> "I was responsible for training new labelers. Many people thought this was easy work, but once they started, they discovered the tasks were difficult, the review standards were strict, and the income was unstable. Many people quit after a few months because they found that after deducting electricity and internet costs, they'd barely earned anything."[^16]

These testimonies come from multiple independent investigative reports. The interviewees requested anonymity because they feared being banned from the platform.

## IV. Impact and Aftermath

**"AI's Foxconn"**:

Critics have compared Scale AI to "AI's Foxconn"—just as Foxconn assembles iPhones for Apple, Scale AI provides labeled data for Silicon Valley's AI companies.[^17] Is this comparison apt?

Similarities:
- **Globalized Division of Labor**: Silicon Valley handles design and sales; developing countries handle manufacturing and labeling
- **Low-Wage Labor**: Labelers' hourly wages ($1–3) are comparable to Foxconn workers' hourly wages ($2–3)
- **High Profit Margins**: Scale AI's profit margins are estimated at 30–50%, comparable to Foxconn[^18]

Differences:
- **Physical Risk**: Foxconn workers face physical risks (prolonged standing, repetitive strain injuries); labelers face psychological risks (prolonged exposure to toxic content)
- **Visibility**: Foxconn workers' situations have been widely reported; labelers' situations still receive little attention
- **Replaceability**: Foxconn workers can be replaced by automation; labelers are currently difficult to fully replace[^19]

**Do Labelers Have the Right to Refuse?**

In theory, Remotasks labelers can stop working at any time—they are "independent contractors," not "employees."[^20] But reality is:

1. **Economic Dependence**: Many labelers have no other income sources; Remotasks is their only option
2. **Information Asymmetry**: Labelers don't know where their labor is used or how much value their labor creates
3. **Bargaining Power**: Labelers are atomized individuals with no unions and no collective bargaining ability[^21]

**Military Contracts**:

Scale AI's collaboration with the US Department of Defense is the most sensitive part of this story.

In 2020, Scale AI obtained a contract from the US Department of Defense to provide data labeling services for the military.[^22] Specific content included:
- Labeling satellite imagery for training military AI
- Labeling drone-captured video for target identification
- Labeling social media data for intelligence analysis[^23]

Scale AI CEO Alexandr Wang stated in an interview:
> "We believe AI can help the United States maintain its military advantage. We're proud to serve the Department of Defense."[^24]

But critics pointed out that Scale AI's military contracts raise serious ethical questions:
- **Do Labelers Know?**: Do Remotasks labelers know that their labeled data might be used for military purposes?
- **Accountability**: If an AI military system malfunctions, who should be responsible? The engineers who designed the AI, or the labelers who labeled the data?
- **Moral Dilemma**: Do labelers have the right to refuse to participate in military projects?[^25]

In 2023, some Remotasks labelers discovered that their labeled data was being used to train military AI. They felt angry and betrayed—they thought they were doing "ordinary" labeling work, never imagining their labor would be used to kill people.[^26]

**Subsequent Developments**:

In 2024, Scale AI's valuation reached $14 billion.[^27] Alexandr Wang became one of the youngest self-made billionaires in the world.[^28]

Meanwhile, Remotasks labelers continue working for $1–3 per hour. Their labor has created tens of billions of dollars in value, but they themselves have gained almost nothing.

Some labelers have begun organizing to demand higher wages and better working conditions.[^29] But Scale AI's response was: they are "independent contractors," not "employees," and the company has no obligation to provide them with any benefits.[^30]

## V. Numbers

**Scale AI's Scale**:

- **Valuation**: $14 billion (2024)[^31]
- **Employee Count**: Approximately 1,000 full-time employees (Silicon Valley headquarters)[^32]
- **Crowdworker Count**: Approximately 100,000–200,000 Remotasks labelers (global)[^33]
- **Client Count**: Over 1,000 enterprise clients[^34]
- **Annual Revenue**: Estimated over $1 billion (2024)[^35]

**Distribution of Remotasks Labelers**:

Estimated distribution of Remotasks labelers by country:[^36]

- **Philippines**: Approximately 50,000–100,000 labelers, Remotasks' largest labeler base
- **Venezuela**: Approximately 20,000–50,000 labelers, many engineers and professionals switching to labeling due to economic crisis
- **Kenya**: Approximately 10,000–30,000 labelers
- **India**: Approximately 10,000–30,000 labelers
- **Other Countries**: Approximately 10,000–50,000 labelers

**Pay Comparison**:

Comparison of Remotasks labelers' hourly wages with local average wages:[^37]

- **Philippines**: Labelers earn $1.5–3/hour, local average $2–4/hour
- **Venezuela**: Labelers earn $1–2/hour, local average $0.5–1/hour (due to economic crisis)
- **Kenya**: Labelers earn $1.5–2.5/hour, local average $1–2/hour
- **United States**: Labelers earn $10–15/hour, local average $25–30/hour

**Profit Structure**:

Scale AI's profit margins are estimated at 30–50%.[^38] This means that of the fees Scale AI charges clients, 30–50% is profit, with the remainder being costs (including labeler wages, server costs, management expenses, etc.).

Using a typical image classification task as an example:[^39]

- **Scale AI charges clients**: $0.10–0.50 per image
- **Scale AI pays labelers**: $0.01–0.05 per image
- **Scale AI's profit margin**: Approximately 80–90%

This profit margin far exceeds most industries. Scale AI's business model is essentially: achieve maximum profit at minimum cost.

## Assessment

The most profitable part of the AI industry isn't making models—it's selling data.

This is a counter-intuitive fact. We assume AI's core is algorithms—Transformers, diffusion models, the technological breakthroughs that make Silicon Valley valuations soar. But Scale AI's story tells us that AI's core is data. Without data, even the best algorithms are just scrap metal.

Scale AI's business model was: transforming data labeling from a "technical problem" into a "labor problem." They didn't care about algorithms or models; they cared about only one thing—how to label the most data at the lowest cost.

The answer: globalization. Outsource the most tedious, repetitive work to the world's cheapest labor force. Labelers in the Philippines earn $1–3 per hour; Silicon Valley engineers earn $100–200 per hour. Scale AI's profit margins hide within this 100-fold gap.

The cruelty of this model lies in: it packages "exploitation" as "opportunity." Remotasks ads say you can work from home, with flexible hours, earning above the local minimum wage. This sounds like a win-win deal—labelers earn money, and Scale AI gets data.

But the essence of this deal is: labelers exchange their labor for a tiny fraction of Scale AI's profits. They've created tens of billions of dollars in value, but gained almost nothing themselves.

The deeper question is: who has the right to determine the value of data?

In Silicon Valley conference rooms, Scale AI executives discuss "data labeling cost structures" and "global labor market efficiency." In Manila rental apartments, labelers work for $1–3 per hour. They don't know where their labor is used or how much value their labor creates.

This information asymmetry is the core of Scale AI's business model. Labelers don't know who they're working for or how much their labor is worth. They only know that for every task completed, they earn a few cents.

Military contracts make this problem even sharper. When labelers discovered their labeled data was being used to train military AI, they felt angry and betrayed. But Scale AI's response was: they are "independent contractors," and the company has no obligation to tell them how the data is used.

This is the latest variant of "technological neutrality." Scale AI would say they merely provide "data labeling services," and how the data is used is the client's concern. But this argument ignores a fact: Scale AI's business model is built upon a global low-wage labor force. Their profits come from labelers' labor.

When we talk about AI's "progress," we should remember: what is the cost of this "progress"? It's labelers in the Philippines earning $1–3 per hour, engineers in Venezuela being forced to switch to labeling, young people in Kenya not knowing their labor is being used for military purposes.

Scale AI's story tells us: the AI industry's wealth doesn't emerge from thin air. It's created by a global low-wage labor force. While Silicon Valley valuations soar, those who provide the "fuel" for AI struggle at the poverty line.

This is not technological progress. This is the latest form of global capitalism. (See "Discourse: The Global South," "Discourse: Labor Justice")
