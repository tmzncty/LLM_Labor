# Two-Sided AI Hiring: When Applicants and Recruiters Both Use Models

> **As of 2026-08-28. Status: REVISED.** Public evidence confirms rapid AI adoption on both sides of hiring, rising application volume, growing automated screening, and systemic risks when the same vendor's algorithms influence many employers. Rising applications cannot be attributed to AI alone, and evidence about one vendor cannot be generalized to every hiring system.

AI changes labor markets before anyone gets hired. Applicants use models to search, draft resumes, prepare interviews, and submit applications; recruiters use models to source, rank, pre-screen, and interview candidates.

## Both sides are automating

LinkedIn reported in January 2026 that U.S. applicants per open role had roughly doubled since spring 2022. It also reported that 81% of surveyed people had used or planned to use AI in their job search, while 93% of recruiters planned to increase AI use in 2026 and 66% planned to increase AI-powered pre-screening interviews.[^1]

These are LinkedIn platform data and surveys. They do **not** establish that AI caused application volume to double. A weak labor market, remote-work competition, and one-click application products also matter.

The narrower conclusion is strong: applicant-side and recruiter-side automation are expanding together.

## Algorithmic monoculture across employers

Stanford's 2026 working paper *Algorithmic Monocultures in Hiring* studies about three million applicants submitting four million applications, all screened by algorithms from the same third-party vendor. The underlying applications primarily date from 2018–2022, so this is evidence about an earlier generation of algorithmic hiring rather than a new 2026 generative-AI interview system.[^2]

Using the U.S. employment-discrimination four-fifths rule as a screening measure, the authors found that 14.74% of applications from Asian applicants and 25.87% of applications from Black applicants were submitted to positions showing adverse impact for those groups.[^2]

**Adverse impact is a regulatory screening indicator, not a court finding of illegal discrimination.**

The study also found that about 4% of applicants who applied to ten positions were recommended for rejection in all ten, more often than expected under independent random outcomes.[^2]

The authors call this **algorithmic monoculture**: when many employers rely on the same vendor or similar models, one algorithm's preferences can become a repeated labor-market barrier rather than a one-company decision.

## AI interviews and disclosure

Greenhouse surveyed 2,950 job seekers in the U.S., UK, Ireland, Germany, and Australia in 2026. In its U.S. sample, 63% said they had experienced an AI interview. Among U.S. candidates with AI-evaluation experience, 70% said AI was not clearly disclosed before their most recent AI interview, and 38% said they had withdrawn from a hiring process because it included an AI interview.[^3]

This is an industry survey, not an official population statistic. But it highlights a governance question: candidates increasingly need to know when a system evaluates them, how much weight its output carries, and whether human review is available.

## Signal inflation

Resumes, cover letters, and first-round interviews used to be costly signals because both sides spent time producing and reading them. Generative AI lowers the cost of producing a plausible application. More applications can therefore coexist with less information in each individual document.

Employers may respond with more screening, skills tests, AI interviews, and greater reliance on referrals or verifiable work. That can create a feedback loop: applicants optimize for machine filters, recruiters add more filters, and human attention becomes the scarce resource.

## What we cannot say

- AI alone caused applications per opening to double.
- Every hiring AI exhibits the disparities found in the Stanford vendor dataset.
- A four-fifths-rule flag is itself a legal discrimination judgment.
- Greenhouse's survey represents all job seekers.
- Algorithmic screening is necessarily worse than human hiring, which also contains bias and inconsistency.

## Analysis

In the emerging hiring market, applicants are not only competing for jobs. They are competing for the limited human attention that remains **after automated screening**.

Labor history should therefore track not only how many recruiters disappear, but who designs the filters, who gets repeatedly filtered out, who can appeal, and how much job-search labor now takes place machine-to-machine.
