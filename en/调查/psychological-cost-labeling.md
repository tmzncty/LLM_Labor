# The Psychological Cost of Data Work: Harmful-Content Moderation Is the High-Risk Case

> **As of 2026-08-28. v2 REVISED.** This revision withdraws the claim that all data labeling carries the same psychological risk. The strongest evidence concerns sustained occupational exposure to violence, sexual abuse, hate, self-harm, and other disturbing material.

## Separate ordinary annotation from harmful-content work

“Data labeling” covers very different jobs. Drawing boxes around road objects, transcribing speech, ranking model answers, and reviewing graphic harmful content do not have the same risk profile.

The OpenAI/Sama reporting in Kenya established that an AI-safety data project included workers classifying harmful text and that interviewed workers described significant psychological distress.[^1] It did **not** establish a population PTSD rate for all AI data workers.

This article therefore addresses a narrower question: occupational exposure to disturbing content.

## What peer-reviewed research shows

A 2024 cross-sectional study by Spence and colleagues found a dose-response relationship between frequency of exposure to distressing content and both psychological distress and secondary trauma among commercial content moderators. Supportive colleagues and meaningful feedback were associated with better outcomes.[^2]

A larger 2025 study by Spence and DeMarco reported that more than a quarter of professional commercial content moderators in its sample had moderate-to-severe psychological distress and roughly a quarter had low well-being. It also replicated associations between harmful-content exposure and poorer psychological outcomes.[^3]

These measures are **not equivalent to a clinical PTSD prevalence rate**. The previous repository article's 65–80% PTSD claim was not adequately sourced and has been removed.

## Can protective measures reduce harm?

Evidence is mixed.

A 2026 experimental study tested visual modifications such as blur and grayscale in simulated moderation tasks and did not find that the tested modifications reduced the measured negative psychological effects under its conditions.[^4]

A 2025 18-month longitudinal study of TaskUs moderators participating in a resilience/support program reported no significant increase in secondary traumatic stress. The authors had employment ties to TaskUs, so the finding should not be generalized, but it is useful counterevidence: organizational support and work design may matter.[^5]

## Why human queues can become harder after automation

Automated systems tend to absorb the most regular cases first. Human reviewers may then receive a higher concentration of ambiguous, disturbing, or high-risk material.

A smaller number of human-reviewed items does not necessarily mean a proportional reduction in psychological load.

## What we do not know

- There is no reliable universal PTSD rate for AI labelers.
- Content-moderation studies cannot automatically be generalized to all RLHF, evaluation, or ordinary data-labeling work.
- Cross-sectional associations do not by themselves prove that every symptom was caused solely by the job.
- Employers differ substantially in shifts, breaks, counseling, rotation, and team support.
- Long-term post-employment health outcomes remain under-studied.

## Analysis

The historical record is more useful when it preserves specific working conditions rather than maximizing shock value. The key questions are who must view harmful material, for how long, whether workers can refuse or rotate away, what support is actually available, and who bears the cost after a project ends.
