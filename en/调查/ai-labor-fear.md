# AI and Labor-Market Fear: Separate Forecasts, Feelings and Observed Change

> **v2 audit: 2026-08-28.** AI labor debates often turn CEO forecasts into facts or treat individual layoffs as economy-wide evidence. By 2026 the empirical picture is clearer: there is no broad economy-wide displacement signal, but young workers in highly AI-exposed occupations show a meaningful employment divergence.

A 2025 Stanford study of 1,500 U.S. workers found concerns about reliability, job loss and insufficient human oversight, while many workers still welcomed automation of low-value tasks and preferred collaborative human-AI arrangements.[^1]

The August 2026 revision of Stanford Digital Economy Lab's ADP-based study found no widespread economy-wide displacement. However, employment for workers ages 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations stood about 19% below where it would have been if it had tracked less-exposed peers; experienced workers showed no comparable gap. The adjustment appeared mainly through reduced hiring rather than increased separations.[^2]

The authors also emphasize uncertainty. Their February 2026 follow-up argues that macro conditions matter and that the strongest timing evidence emerges mainly from 2024 onward.[^3]

The defensible conclusion is therefore not “AI has already destroyed millions of jobs.” It is that evidence is increasingly consistent with meaningful AI pressure on some entry-level labor markets.

Forecasts about “half of white-collar entry jobs” or a “permanent underclass” should be archived as forecasts and rhetoric, not employment outcomes.

## Evidence boundaries

- 19% is a relative employment gap, not “19% laid off by AI.”
- The Stanford evidence is U.S.-specific.
- No aggregate displacement does not mean no sectoral disruption.
- Worker fear is not a forecast.
- CEO predictions remain predictions.
