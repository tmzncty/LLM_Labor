# Venezuela Labelers

> Venezuela experienced "the worst peacetime economic disaster in nearly 50 years," coinciding with the explosion in global data labeling demand. Approximately 200,000 Venezuelans flooded crowdsourcing platforms, comprising 75% of the global workforce on some platforms. They支撑起了 the US AI industry's data pipeline with their meager美元-denominated income. And when generative AI began吞噬 low-skill labeling work, this lifeline began断裂.

## I. Background

Venezuela's economic collapse is one of the most剧烈 peacetime economic disasters of the 21st century. Hyperinflation destroyed the purchasing power of the bolivar, leaving millions unemployed or被迫移民.[^1] But unlike many developing countries, Venezuela retained相当水平 internet infrastructure—electricity and broadband connections remained available in urban areas.[^2]

This combination of "economic collapse + remaining infrastructure"恰好 collided with the global AI industry's demand for cheap labeling labor. Professor Florian Alexander Schmidt (弗洛里安·亚历山大·施密特) of HTW Dresden said: "Venezuela's example clearly shows how the combination of poverty and good infrastructure makes such phenomena possible. As the crisis shifts globally, there will likely be another country that can play the same role."[^1]

**Scale AI and Remotasks' Global布局**

Scale AI was founded in 2016 by 19-year-old MIT student Alexandr Wang (亚历山大·王), with clients including Toyota Research, Lyft, and OpenAI.[^1] In 2017, Scale launched its worker-side platform **Remotasks**, building a global pool of cheap contractors.[^1] Initially recruiting in the Philippines and Kenya, it entered Venezuela in 2019.[^1]

In 2019, after Uber acquired Mighty AI and restricted Spare5 access,大量 labelers flooded into Remotasks.[^1] In early 2020, Scale created a Venezuela-specific login page, launched **Remotasks Plus** (invitation-only, promising minimum hourly wages and bonuses), and the pandemic further drove a surge in registrations.[^1]

**Competitive Landscape**: Scale dominated high-end startup clients, Appen served giants like Google, YouTube, and Facebook, and Hive Micro served low-end clients. By mid-2018, approximately 200,000 Venezuelans had registered on Hive Micro and Spare5, each comprising 75% of their global workforce.[^1]

## II. Core Facts

**Three Pillars**

Yale University researcher Julian Posada's (朱利安·波萨达) study指出 that Venezuela became an AI data hub for three reasons:[^2]

1. **Infrastructure exists**: Electricity and internet connections remain available
2. **Currency collapse**: Hyperinflation made the bolivar极度 unstable, and the US dollar became the de facto currency—crowdsourcing platforms恰好 pay in dollars
3. **High unemployment**:大量 educated people seeking online work paid in dollars

**Job Content**

Venezuelan labelers' work goes far beyond "tagging." According to Posada's research, their tasks include:[^2]

- **Generating data**: Taking selfies for facial recognition training
- **Labeling data**: Marking objects like windows, lamps, and paintings in kitchen photos
- **Verifying data**: Telling machines "good job" or "bad job"
- **Red teaming**: Attempting to make algorithms output illegal or exploitative content to find security vulnerabilities

**The Intermediary Extraction Machine**

This is the cruelest finding in Posada's research. Labelers' income doesn't go directly from platform to pocket—it passes through multiple intermediary layers: platform → PayPal → electronic wallet → bolivar exchange broker.[^2] In a normal economy, more participants might benefit actors, but in freelancers' cases, **the more participants, the lower workers' income**. Each intermediary layer收取 commission, and workers lose autonomy.

Posada calls this phenomenon "deep embeddedness" (深度嵌入)—economic behavior is deeply intertwined with social relations and structures.[^2] His original words:

> "The erosion of worker autonomy, particularly regarding economic compensation, highlights the overall deterioration of working conditions in the gig economy."[^2]

**Oskarina Fuentes's Story**

MIT Technology Review's investigative report told the story of a specific person: Oskarina Fuentes Anaya (奥斯卡丽娜·富恩特斯·阿纳亚).[^1] She was originally a petroleum engineering student; Venezuela's economic collapse forced her to中断 studies and work full-time on Appen data labeling. She later移居 Colombia but still works for American AI company platforms.

Fuentes's story is a microcosm of hundreds of thousands of Venezuelan labelers: educated, with professional skills, but economic crisis pushed them to the very bottom of the global AI supply chain. They didn't "choose" labeling work—they had no other choice.

**200,000 People, 75% Share**

- On platforms like Hive Micro and Spare5, Venezuelan workers comprise **approximately 75%** of the global workforce

## IV. Impact and Aftermath

**Generative AI's Backfire**

The most ironic twist comes from Rest of World's 2025 report: generative AI is replacing the low-skill labeling work Venezuelans depend on for survival.[^5] AI not only relied on Venezuela's cheap labor to train itself, but is now beginning to吞噬 these workers' livelihoods.

The report指出 that complex AI training and testing still requires human labor, but this work is "not easily accessible"—requiring higher education levels and skills, with higher barriers, and only一部分 Venezuelan labelers can胜任.[^5] Low-skill labeling, classification, and verification work is being replaced by automation tools.

**Scale AI's Supply Cut**

ComputerWorld reported that Scale AI切断 access for remote workers in several countries, with workers suddenly losing income sources without transition periods.[^6] For Venezuelan families who made labeling their sole income source, this meant归零 overnight.

**Brazil's Parallel Narrative**

As Latin America's largest economy, Brazil is also developing data labeling labor. A Pulitzer Center investigation found that Brazil's Ministry of Labor and Employment established a working group to discuss data work, but data labor issues **were not even included in the AI regulatory bill approved by the Senate in November 2024**.[^7] This means Brazil's AI regulatory framework ignored labelers from its inception—the most vulnerable link in the AI supply chain.

Privacy International's report指出 that data labeling includes drawing bounding boxes, classifying social media post sentiment, evaluating video clips, and other work; how labelers are managed is a systemic issue.[^8] LLM developers' response is "scaling up training methods"—faster, smarter—rather than improving labor conditions.

**Academic Attention**

Julian Posada's research (published in Big Data & Society and New Technology, Work and Employment) is one of the most important academic outputs in this field.[^2][^4] He proposed "deep embeddedness" theory, revealing how intermediary chains in labeling labor systematically erode workers' income. The Data Workers' Inquiry project had data workers from 19 countries conduct research in their own workplaces, self-reporting labor conditions.[^9]

**Structural Dilemma**

The situation of Venezuelan labelers reveals the core paradox of Global South labeling labor:

1. **Crisis as Market**: Economic disaster created大量 cheap, educated, internet-connected labor—exactly what the AI industry needs
2. **Platform as Rule-maker**: Platforms set the rules of the game, and workers can only accept—prices, task分配, access and exit are all determined by platforms
3. **Intermediaries as Tax**: Every step from dollars to bolivars抽取 workers' income
4. **AI as Replacement**: The AI trained by labeling workers is replacing labeling workers themselves

This forms a Latin American counterpart to the Kenyan labelers case (see "Investigation: Kenya Labelers"):同样是 Global South cheap labor serving Silicon Valley's AI industry,同样是 structural power asymmetry,同样是 platforms profiting during crisis and撤离 after crisis ends. The difference is: Kenyan labelers' pain is primarily psychological (reading toxic content), while Venezuelan labelers' pain is primarily economic (being squeezed by intermediary layers, ultimately replaced by AI).

## Assessment

The story of Venezuelan labelers is the Latin American version of Global South labeling labor—a mirror image of Kenyan labelers (see "Investigation: Kenya Labelers"), but with essential differences.

Kenyan labelers' pain was "seen"—Time's investigative report made their mental health issues a global topic. Venezuelan labelers' pain is more隐蔽: not the psychological trauma of toxic content, but systematic economic structure exploitation. Their income travels from the platform, through PayPal, electronic wallets, and bolivar exchange brokers, with each layer skimming. Julian Posada used "deep embeddedness" theory to describe this phenomenon: the more intermediaries, the poorer workers become. In normal economic theory, more participants mean more choice and competition; in labeling labor reality, more participants mean more extraction and less autonomy.

The most ironic element is generative AI's backfire. Venezuelans flooded into the labeling industry because of economic crisis—they had no other choice. They trained AI models with their meager incomes. And these models are now replacing low-skill labeling work. This isn't "creative destruction"—this is "destructive destruction." Labelers went from being "AI's fuel" to "AI's waste" in less than a decade.

Scale AI's story exposes the cold logic of platform economics. Entering Venezuela in 2019, launching dedicated pages and invitation-only platforms in 2020, registrations surging during the pandemic—all calculated market expansion. And when the platform needs to "optimize," it can cut off access for workers in multiple countries overnight, without transition, without compensation. A $7.3 billion valuation built on the labor of 200,000 Venezuelan workers, but these workers can't even get severance pay.

Brazil's case reveals another dimension of neglect: AI regulatory legislation ignored data labor from its inception. This isn't oversight—it's a structural blind spot. Legislators focus on AI's outputs (bias, privacy, security), not AI's inputs (who's labeling, under what conditions, for what compensation). Labelers are the invisible people in the AI supply chain.

Posada's "networked agency" concept provides a glimmer of hope: Venezuelan workers are not completely被动; they build互助 mechanisms through social networks, fighting for certain autonomy under严苛 conditions. But this individual-level resistance cannot change structural power asymmetry. When platforms set the rules of the game, workers' "agency" is at best survival in the cracks.

Venezuela's lesson is: Global South labeling labor is not a "transitional phase"—it's a meticulously designed exploitation structure. Crisis created cheap labor, platforms harvested this labor, AI replaced this labor. The beneficiaries of this entire cycle are Silicon Valley companies and investors; the costs are borne by the most vulnerable people.
