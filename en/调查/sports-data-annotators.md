# Sports Data Annotators: The Human Scoring Desk Behind AI Football

> **As of 2026-08-28. Evidence status: human data work around the 2026 World Cup is documented by reporting; there is no unified public measure of the global sports-annotation workforce.**

Broadcast graphics showing player trajectories, automated offside decisions, and “AI insights” can look like direct outputs of sensors and algorithms. Sports analytics, however, has long depended on people watching video and turning continuous play into structured events.

Rest of World reported in June 2026 that workers in countries including Brazil, Cambodia, the Philippines, and India were manually creating and checking data used in football analytics around the World Cup.[^1]

## What the work looks like

Annotators may record:

- which player touched the ball;
- when and where an event occurred;
- whether an action was a pass, tackle, shot, or another event;
- trajectories and contextual tags;
- corrections to automated detections.

The resulting data can serve teams, broadcasters, video platforms, games, and betting products.[^1]

## This labor predates generative AI

Human sports coding existed long before the current generative-AI boom. Clubs and data companies already employed people to classify events and analyze video. Machine vision and predictive models are being layered on top of that existing data-production system.

This matters because not all “AI labor” trains chatbots. Many industries already had workers whose role was to translate physical reality into structured data. AI increases the value and scale of that data.

## Why humans remain in the loop

Sports video contains occlusion, camera changes, ambiguous event boundaries, and customer-specific definitions. Human work can therefore appear at three points:

1. before deployment, to produce labeled training data;
2. during operations, to fill gaps or correct real-time systems;
3. after operations, for quality control and reclassification.

Automation may shift workers from direct recording toward exception handling rather than eliminate them immediately.

## Geographic division of labor

Rest of World described a value chain in which higher-value analytics can be concentrated inside wealthy clubs and markets while repetitive video processing and annotation are distributed internationally.[^1]

That resembles other AI data supply chains: high-margin analysis and product design are concentrated, while granular data preparation can be outsourced across lower-cost regions.

## What we do not know

- There is no complete public count of global sports data annotators.
- Pay and contract conditions differ by vendor and country.
- AI adoption does not by itself prove that annotation employment will fall; more automated systems can also create more data requiring review.
- Team analytics, broadcasting, games, and betting may share data but have different commercial models and labor requirements.

## Analysis

AI does not directly “see” a football match in the human sense. A large part of machine-readable understanding depends on someone first deciding how reality should be divided into events.

Sports data workers are therefore a useful window into a broader pattern found in autonomous vehicles, medical imaging, remote sensing, retail video, and robotics: before machines can learn from the world, people often have to describe the world in a standardized form.
