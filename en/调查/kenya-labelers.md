# Kenyan Labelers: Harmful-Content Data Work Behind Generative-AI Safety

> **As of 2026-08-28. Evidence status: the core facts are supported by high-quality investigative reporting; long-term health outcomes and industry-wide generalizations require tighter evidence.**

In 2023, a TIME investigation made a previously obscure layer of AI labor visible. Before ChatGPT launched, OpenAI had contracted with Sama for workers in Kenya to read and classify text containing violence, hate, and descriptions of sexual abuse as part of a safety-data project.[^1]

The case matters not because it represents all RLHF or all AI safety work, but because it demonstrates a recurring structure: software capabilities can depend on large amounts of human judgment, while the most disturbing and psychologically risky parts of the work are easy to outsource.

## What we know

### OpenAI and Sama had a real contract

TIME reported that OpenAI contracted with Sama in 2021 for Kenyan workers to classify harmful text, including sexual abuse, hate speech, and violence.[^1]

The work was related to model safety, but it should not be described as “Kenyan workers trained all of ChatGPT's safety.” Generative-AI safety also involves model training, policies, evaluation, filtering, red teaming, and other systems. What the case establishes is narrower: **a meaningful safety-data project depended on human harmful-content labeling.**

### Workers took home less than $2 per hour

TIME reported that take-home pay across worker levels was roughly $1.32–$2 per hour and that OpenAI's contract rate to Sama was substantially higher than worker take-home pay.[^1]

Client payment, contractor labor cost, and worker take-home pay are different numbers and must not be collapsed into one “AI annotation wage.”

### The work involved serious psychological burden

Workers interviewed by TIME described sustained exposure to extreme harmful material and significant psychological distress.[^1]

The evidence supports the existence of severe psychological burden and worker-reported harm. Without medical diagnosis or systematic epidemiological data, the archive should not label every worker as having PTSD or infer a population PTSD rate.

### Sama ended the relevant work early

TIME reported that Sama ended the OpenAI-related contracts in February 2022, earlier than originally planned, in the context of controversy over harmful-content work and worker well-being.[^1]

The early end can be documented. The complete set of internal motives cannot be reconstructed beyond what the companies and reporting disclosed.

## From exposure to collective organization

The Kenyan story later became part of a larger labor-organizing story. In 2023, more than 150 African content moderators and AI workers associated with different outsourcing operations gathered in Nairobi and voted to form a cross-company organization around pay, mental health, working conditions, and organizing rights.[^2]

Richard Mathenge became a visible organizer in this movement. TIME later profiled him as a former worker on the OpenAI-related harmful-content project who helped form the African Content Moderators Union and joined a petition to Kenya's parliament.[^3]

This changes the historical record. The workers were not only subjects of harm; some began trying to redefine fragmented contractors as a labor group with common interests.

## What we do not know

1. This case does not establish that Kenyan labelers produced all of ChatGPT's safety training.
2. It does not establish that every RLHF project or model company uses the same pay and conditions.
3. A few interviewees cannot establish a population PTSD rate.
4. Outsourcing itself is not proof of exploitation; pay, benefits, risk compensation, stability, and bargaining power require project-level comparison.
5. Wages reported for 2021–2022 should not be presented as 2026 industry rates.

## Analysis

The strongest reason to preserve this case is not the slogan that “AI is built on human suffering.” It is the traceable production chain. Once the chain is visible, readers can examine who performed the risky work, who set the price, who held the client relationship, and where responsibility stopped.
