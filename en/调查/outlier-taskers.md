# Outlier Taskers: Contract Labor in the Post-Training Era

> **As of 2026-08-28. Evidence status: worker classification and task categories are documented in platform terms; work experiences and disputes rely mainly on reported interviews and should remain attributed.**

Scale AI was once best known for Remotasks. In the post-training era, Outlier became a more visible platform for contributors with university-level or advanced domain expertise. Workers may generate prompts, compare model responses, rewrite answers, and perform specialized evaluations.

This is not a conventional permanent “AI trainer” job. It is platform-mediated contract labor.

## How the platform defines the relationship

Outlier's Terms of Use, effective May 11, 2026, state that task contributors are independent contractors rather than employees and do not receive employee benefits.[^1]

Its FAQ says contributors may generate prompts, rank outputs, improve model responses, or perform other tasks intended to improve model performance. Projects vary with customer demand and are not guaranteed to continue.[^2]

Together, these rules describe a clear labor model: the platform supplies access to projects, contributors sell judgment, and work availability depends on downstream customers.

## From crowdwork to expert crowdwork

Earlier crowdwork systems often simplified tasks so that very large pools of workers could complete them. Outlier publicly emphasizes domain expertise, with many opportunities requiring undergraduate-level knowledge and preferring graduate-level credentials.[^3]

As simple judgments become easier to automate, stronger models create demand for workers who can detect subtle errors in mathematics, programming, law, science, writing, and agent workflows.

## Reported disputes and working conditions

A Guardian investigation in April 2026 interviewed Outlier taskers who described a wide range of projects and raised complaints about unstable task availability, changing rates, monitoring, qualification tests, and disturbing or ethically uncomfortable work.[^4]

Those accounts cannot be generalized to every contributor or project. They do, however, match a structural feature visible in the contract: when work is project-based, the contributor absorbs much of the risk that work may disappear.

## Scale and Meta

Reuters reported in June 2025 that Meta invested about $14.3 billion in Scale AI for roughly 49% of the company. Scale said publicly that it remained operationally independent.[^5][^6]

That ownership relationship does **not** establish that any specific Outlier task serves Meta. Platform ownership, customer relationships, and individual projects are separate facts.

## Common uncertainties for workers

- having an account does not guarantee tasks;
- projects may require additional qualification tests;
- rates differ by expertise, location, and project;
- workers may not know the final model or customer;
- identity and location rules affect account access;
- contractor status generally lacks traditional employee benefits.

## What we do not know

- There is no independent public estimate of typical platform-wide hourly earnings.
- Negative media cases do not represent every contributor.
- High advertised rates do not represent most projects.
- Task descriptions alone cannot establish which model receives the data.
- Meta's investment does not identify the customer of a specific task.

## Analysis

Platforms specialize in turning continuous occupations into discrete transactions: rides, deliveries, and now judgments. Outlier is historically interesting because the item being sold is often a small unit of professional reasoning.

Whether this becomes a stable occupation is unknown. But it is already mature enough to archive as a distinct labor form.
