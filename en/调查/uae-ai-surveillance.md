# UAE AI Surveillance

> The UAE is building the world's most advanced AI surveillance system. Dubai's Oyoon project integrates over 300,000 cameras with facial recognition capabilities; Abu Dhabi's Falcon Eye system achieves comprehensive city-wide monitoring. National Security Advisor Sheikh Tahnoon controls over $1.5 trillion in assets, transforming Abu Dhabi into an AI superpower through entities like G42. But what is the cost? The complete loss of citizen privacy, the systematic suppression of free speech, and a digital Leviathan constructed with AI technology.

## I. Background

The UAE, particularly Abu Dhabi and Dubai, is undergoing a silent revolution. This is not a revolution of oil wealth, but an AI-driven surveillance revolution.

In 2017, the UAE appointed the world's first Minister of Artificial Intelligence—Omar Sultan Al Olama (奥马尔·苏丹·阿尔奥拉马).[^1] This was not a symbolic position. The UAE's National AI Strategy 2031 explicitly positions AI as the core of national competitiveness, aiming to increase AI's contribution to GDP to 14% by 2031.[^2]

But the UAE's AI strategy has a notable characteristic: it is heavily focused on surveillance and security applications. This is no coincidence—in a country where political power is highly concentrated, AI surveillance technology is the optimal tool for maintaining control.

**Key Figure: Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed Al Nahyan (谢赫·塔赫农·本·扎耶德·阿勒纳哈扬)**

Sheikh Tahnoon is the brother of the UAE's current president and serves as National Security Advisor.[^3] He controls a vast commercial empire with assets exceeding $1.5 trillion. His investment portfolio includes:

- **G42**: The UAE's largest AI company, which formed a $1.5 billion strategic partnership with Microsoft in 2024[^4]
- **Royal Group**: A diversified investment conglomerate spanning finance, real estate, and energy
- **International Holding Company (IHC)**: One of the UAE's largest publicly listed companies

Sheikh Tahnoon's role reveals a core characteristic of the UAE's AI surveillance system: **the deep integration of national security and commercial interests**. G42 is both a commercial AI company and part of the national security apparatus. In a democracy, this fusion would raise serious conflict-of-interest concerns, but in the UAE, it is the normal mode of state operations.

## II. Core Facts

### Dubai's Oyoon Project

"Oyoon" means "eyes" in Arabic. The project lives up to its name.[^5]

**Scale**:
- Integrates over 300,000 cameras
- Covers all public areas in Dubai
- 24-hour real-time monitoring

**Technical Capabilities**:
- **Facial Recognition**: Real-time identification and tracking of individuals
- **Behavioral Analysis**: Detection of anomalous behavior such as loitering, running, gathering
- **License Plate Recognition**: Automatic recording of all vehicle movements
- **Crowd Density Monitoring**: Prediction and prevention of crowd gatherings

**How It Works**:
The Oyoon system is not merely "recording." It is an intelligent surveillance network that can:
1. Analyze video streams in real-time
2. Identify specific individuals (through facial recognition)
3. Track individuals' movement trajectories throughout the city
4. Predict potential security threats
5. Automatically trigger alerts

### Abu Dhabi's Falcon Eye System

Falcon Eye is Abu Dhabi's comprehensive city-wide surveillance system.[^6] Similar to Oyoon but larger in scale and more integrated.

**Features**:
- Covers the entire city of Abu Dhabi
- Deeply integrated with traffic management systems
- Real-time connection to national security databases
- Supports multi-department coordination (police, transportation, municipal services)

### Legal Framework for AI Surveillance

The UAE's AI surveillance is built upon a series of legal foundations:[^7]

**Federal Law No. 34/2021 (Combating Rumors and Cybercrime)**:
- Criminalizes online activities that oppose the basic principles of governance
- Maximum penalty: Life imprisonment
- Used to suppress academics, journalists, and activists

**Key Provisions**:
- Article 26: Using information technology to harm national unity, social peace, or public order
- Article 29: Spreading false information or rumors
- Article 44: Unauthorized access to information systems

These legal provisions provide the legal basis for AI surveillance. When the surveillance system detects "anomalous" behavior, law enforcement agencies can take action based on these laws.

## III. Testimonies

**Ahmed Mansoor (艾哈迈德·曼苏尔)**:

Ahmed Mansoor was one of the UAE's most prominent human rights defenders. In 2017, he discovered that Pegasus spyware developed by Israel's NSO Group was monitoring his phone.[^8]

Mansoor did not remain silent. He publicly exposed the surveillance and continued to speak out for human rights. In 2018, he was arrested by UAE authorities on charges of "spreading false information and rumors through social media."[^9]

In 2021, Mansoor was sentenced to 10 years in prison.[^10] His case became a标志性案例 of the conflict between AI surveillance and human rights in the UAE.

**Princess Latifa (拉蒂法公主)**:

Princess Latifa is the daughter of Dubai's ruler, Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum.[^11] In 2018, she attempted to flee Dubai but was intercepted in the Indian Ocean and brought back.

According to the BBC, Princess Latifa's phone was植入 Pegasus spyware during her captivity.[^12] Her case reveals how AI surveillance technology can be used to control individuals—even members of the royal family are not spared.

**Anonymous Activist**:

An activist who wished to remain anonymous told a human rights organization:[^13]

> "We live in a glass house. We know we're being watched, but we don't know to what extent. I can't say anything critical of the government on WhatsApp because I don't know who's watching. I can't attend any gathering because cameras will record my face. This isn't freedom; this is a digital prison."

### International Response

**COP28 Surveillance Concerns**:

The 2023 United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP28) was held in Dubai.[^14] Human rights organizations expressed concern that extensive surveillance measures might侵犯 participants' privacy rights and威胁 open dialogue and conference success.

**Pegasus Spyware Scandal**:

In December 2024, a court ruled that NSO Group was liable for attacks on approximately 1,400 WhatsApp users, with compensation exceeding $167 million.[^15] These victims included activists and journalists believed to have been surveilled by the UAE and other Middle Eastern countries.

## Assessment

The UAE's AI surveillance system is a meticulously constructed digital Leviathan. (See "Discourse: Surveillance Capitalism")

This system has three characteristics. First, it is technologically advanced. 300,000 cameras, facial recognition, behavioral analysis, predictive policing—these are not science fiction; they are the reality on Dubai's streets. Second, it is legally comprehensive. Federal Law No. 34/2021 provides the legal basis for surveillance, making everything "legal." Third, power is concentrated. Sheikh Tahnoon alone controls $1.5 trillion in assets and AI giants like G42, with deep integration of national security and commercial interests.

These three characteristics combined create a new type of surveillance state. Traditional surveillance states rely on human agents—secret police, informants, archives. The UAE's surveillance state relies on AI—cameras, algorithms, data. Human surveillance has limits; AI surveillance does not. One person can only监视 a few individuals; one AI system can simultaneously monitor millions.

The deeper question is: is this surveillance "for security" or "for control"?

The UAE government would say these surveillance systems are for combating crime, preventing terrorism, and maintaining public safety. These goals are not inherently problematic. But when surveillance systems are used to suppress dissent,压制 free speech, and imprison human rights defenders, "security" becomes a euphemism for "control."

Ahmed Mansoor's case is a microcosm of this problem. He committed no crime, engaged in no violence, no terrorism. His "offense": using his phone to expose the government's surveillance activities. Then he was captured by the surveillance system, convicted by the legal system, and sent to prison.

This is a perfect closed loop: the surveillance system identifies "threats," the legal system convicts "threats," the prison system eliminates "threats." In this loop, the definition of "threat" is entirely controlled by the government. Anyone who criticizes the government can be defined as a "threat."

The UAE's AI surveillance system also reveals a global trend: **the democratization of surveillance technology and the concentration of surveillance power**.

AI surveillance technology is spreading globally. From predictive policing in the United States to China's Skynet project, from facial recognition in the UK to Dubai's Oyoon project—surveillance technology is everywhere. But the distribution of surveillance power is uneven. In democracies, surveillance power is constrained by law, media, and civil society. In authoritarian states, surveillance power is virtually unconstrained.

The UAE's case is particularly值得关注 because it demonstrates how an authoritarian state can use the most advanced AI technology to construct a surveillance system. This system not only monitors its own citizens but also foreign visitors—including representatives attending COP28.

When we talk about AI's "progress," we should ask: whom does this "progress" serve? Citizens' safety, or government control? Individual freedom, or state power?

The UAE's answer is clear: AI serves the state, and the state serves the ruler. In this equation, citizens' privacy and freedom are expendable costs.

This is not technological progress. This is the collusion of technology and power.

In discussions about global AI governance, the UAE's case poses a sharp question: if a country uses AI to build a perfect surveillance system, what should other countries do? Learn from its technology, or condemn its human rights record?

The answer depends on what you believe: technology is neutral, or technology has values?

If technology is neutral, then the UAE's surveillance technology can be used by any country—as long as it's "legal." But if technology has values, then the UAE's surveillance technology embodies a particular set of values: **security above freedom, control above rights**.

These values are spreading globally through AI technology. When we praise the UAE's "smart cities," we should remember: the flip side of smart cities is digital prisons.
